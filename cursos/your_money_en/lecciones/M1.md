# M1 U01 | Is all the money I receive money I earned?
objetivo: Tell apart the money you earn, the money you borrow, the money you only move and the money you get back.
gancho: Alex checks the bank account on a Friday and sees more money than ever. It looks like a good week. But part of that money isn't really Alex's: it's an advance that will come out of the next paychecks. In this lesson you'll learn to recognize which money is truly yours before you spend it.

== esencial

--- paso | fa-sign-in | Not everything that comes in is income
When you see a deposit in your account or get cash, it's easy to think you earned that money.

That's not always true. Sometimes someone lent it to you. Sometimes you only moved it from one place to another. And sometimes you got back something you had already paid.

Knowing where each deposit comes from helps you avoid spending money you'll have to give back later.

--- tarjetas | fa-th-large | The four kinds of money coming in
* fa-briefcase | {{Earned income|Money you are paid for your work, or what is left from your sales. It's yours to spend.}} | Wages, tips or the profit from your sales. | It's yours to spend. | si
* fa-handshake-o | {{Loan|Money someone gives you today that you have to pay back later, sometimes with an extra cost.}} | An advance from your boss, a loan from a friend or an advance from an app. | You'll have to pay it back. | no
* fa-exchange | Moving money between your accounts | Moving money from savings to checking, or taking cash out of an ATM. | It isn't new money: it only changed places. | igual
* fa-undo | {{Refund|Money you get back for something you already paid, like when you return a pair of shoes.}} | Money back for a purchase, or a charge the bank corrected. | It only recovers money you already spent. | igual

--- ecuacion | fa-balance-scale | What is really yours
Imagine you have 100 dollars and someone lends you 50. Now you can use 150, but you still owe 50.
= 150 | What you have
- 50 | What you owe
= 100 | What is yours
What you have minus what you owe is called your **{{net worth|What is really yours: everything you have minus everything you owe.}}**.
> **Key idea:** a loan gives you money to use today, but it doesn't make you richer.

--- paso | fa-exclamation-circle | Why it matters
If you count a loan as if it were wages, you may commit to expenses your job doesn't cover.

The problem doesn't show up that day. It shows up weeks later, when the advance comes out of your paycheck or the loan is due.

> **Case in one minute:** Alex gets a 400-dollar advance on wages. If that money goes to a refrigerator, the next paychecks will be 400 smaller and rent will come up short. The advance wasn't new money: it was Alex's own future pay.

--- paso | fa-search | Signs that a deposit isn't income
Take a closer look at a deposit if:

- it comes from a {{payroll advance|Apps or employers that give you part of your future pay today. It comes out of your next paycheck, and sometimes there's a fee.}} app, or from your boss "against" your wages;
- someone tells you they lent it to you or "deposited it by mistake";
- it's a transfer from another account of yours;
- your statement says *refund*, *reversal* or *credit adjustment*.

> **Before you act, check:** if you don't recognize a deposit, mark it "to be cleared up." Don't spend it until you know what it is.

--- pasos | fa-check-square-o | Do it this week
1. Open your account activity for the last month.
2. Mark each deposit with a letter: **E** (earned income), **L** (loan), **M** (money moved between your accounts) or **R** (refund).
3. Add up only the deposits marked **E**. That's your real income for the month.
4. For each **L**, write down how much you owe and when you'll pay it.

--- comprueba
1. An app deposits a 200 advance. Is it earned income? || No. It's a loan: it will come out of your next paycheck.
2. You move 100 from savings to checking. Do you have more money? || No. It only changed places. The total is the same.

--- recuerda
- Before you spend a large deposit, ask yourself where it came from.
- If it's a loan, write down when and how you'll pay it back.
- If you don't know what it is, don't spend it yet.

== profundiza

--- tema | fa-money | What money is for
Money has three jobs:

1. **Exchange:** buying and selling without trading one thing for another.
2. **Pricing things:** knowing whether something is expensive or cheap.
3. **Storing buying power:** saving today to use later.

--- tema | fa-sitemap | Money, income and wealth are not the same
**Money** is what you can use to pay: cash or an account balance.

**Income** is what you receive in exchange for your work or your sales.

**Wealth**, or net worth, is everything you have minus everything you owe.

A person can own a house and have almost no cash: they have wealth, but little money available. Another person can have a lot of cash because someone lent it to them: they have money, but no more wealth.

--- tema | fa-credit-card | The forms money takes
A bill is **{{cash|Bills and coins you have in your hand.}}**.

A balance at the bank is a **{{deposit|Money you keep at a bank or credit union. They owe it to you and give it back when you ask.}}**: the bank owes you that money.

A **{{debit|A card that pays with your own money from your account.}}** card pays with your deposit. A **{{credit|A card that pays with money the bank lends you; if you don't pay it all on time, they charge interest.}}** card pays with a loan.

An app can show you several balances on the same screen. Before you use it, find out who holds your money. You can check whether a bank is insured on [FDIC BankFind](https://banks.data.fdic.gov/bankfind-suite/) and whether a credit union is insured on [NCUA](https://mapping.ncua.gov).

> **Good to know:** in the U.S., money at an FDIC-insured bank is protected up to 250,000 dollars. The limit is per person, per bank and per type of account. At insured credit unions, the NCUA gives the same protection.

--- tema | fa-clock-o | Payroll advances
Some apps and some employers give you today part of the pay you've already worked for. It comes out of your next paycheck.

Even if they say "no interest," they can have costs: a fee to get the money instantly, or a "tip" the app suggests.

If you use advances often, each paycheck arrives a little shorter. Use them only for an emergency and write down the date they'll be taken out.

--- tabla | fa-list-ol | Step by step
Follow how your cash and your net worth change with each move.

| Step | What happens | Cash | Things you own | Debts | Net worth |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | You start with no debts | 500 | 0 | 0 | 500 |
| 2 | You get your 300 paycheck | 800 | 0 | 0 | 800 |
| 3 | You buy a 200 tool | 600 | 200 | 0 | 800 |
| 4 | You spend 50 on food | 550 | 200 | 0 | 750 |
| 5 | Someone lends you 100 | 650 | 200 | 100 | 750 |
| 6 | The tool is now worth 120 | 650 | 120 | 100 | 670 |

In step 5 you have more cash, but your net worth didn't change.

In step 6 your net worth went down 80, even though you didn't spend anything that day.

--- tema | fa-shopping-basket | If you work for yourself
Not everything you charge is profit. Part of it pays you back for materials, merchandise or transportation.

> **Key idea:** profit = what you charge − what you spent to be able to sell.

If you don't do this subtraction, you may spend as profit the money you need to buy next week's merchandise.

--- casos
### Case 1. The advance from the boss
Alex gets a 400-dollar advance on wages. That week the account shows more money than ever. Mar wants to use it to buy a refrigerator.
? What should they consider? || The advance will come out of the next paychecks. It isn't new income.
? What happens if they spend it today? || In the next few weeks they'll have 400 less for rent and food.

### Case 2. Daniela's cakes
Daniela sold 600 dollars' worth of cakes in a month. She spent 250 on ingredients and boxes.
? How much did she earn? || 350 before taxes, not 600.
? What happens if she spends the 600? || Next month she won't have money to buy ingredients.

### Case 3. The mystery deposit
A 300 deposit shows up in Rubén's account. He doesn't know where it came from. A few days later, someone texts him: "I deposited it by mistake, send it back to me by Zelle."
? What should Rubén do? || Not spend it and not send it back on his own. First he should call his bank at its official number.
? Why? || It may be a scam. In many of these scams the original deposit is reversed later, and the money you "send back" comes out of your own balance. You can report it at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov).

--- errores
* Counting an advance or a loan as wages | You commit to expenses your job doesn't cover | Write down the loan and its due date
* Counting everything you sell as profit | You run out of money to restock | Subtract your costs first
* Thinking that moving money between accounts means having more | Your budget looks bigger than it is | Count the money in all your accounts only once
* Spending a deposit you don't recognize | It may be a mistake or a scam, and they can take it back later | Mark it "to be cleared up" and call the bank

# M1 U02 | Why doesn't the same money go as far anymore?
objetivo: Calculate percentages, inflation, and simple and compound interest, and use them to understand your decisions.
gancho: Mar buys the same groceries she bought a year ago and pays more. Her pay hasn't changed. She didn't overspend: money is worth less than before. In this lesson you'll learn to measure how much prices go up and how much borrowed money costs.

== esencial

--- ecuacion | fa-shopping-cart | When prices go up
Mar's groceries used to cost 100 dollars. Now they cost 105.
= 105 | Today's price
- 100 | Earlier price
= 5 | Up 5 out of every 100: 5%
When prices go up in general, it's called **{{inflation|When almost everything costs more than before. The same money buys less.}}**.

--- paso | fa-balance-scale | Your buying power
If prices go up 5% and your income doesn't, you can buy less with the same money.

That's called losing **{{buying power|What your money can buy. If prices go up and your pay doesn't, it goes down.}}**.

It isn't your fault. It's a reason to review your budget from time to time, not only when something goes wrong.

> **Key idea:** compare your own prices. The inflation in the news is an average; your rent or your food can go up more.

--- paso | fa-percent | How to calculate a percentage
"Percent" means "out of every hundred."

To find 5% of 800, turn 5% into 0.05 and multiply: 800 × 0.05 = 40.

To find how much something changed: (new price − earlier price) ÷ earlier price. From 80 to 100 is (100 − 80) ÷ 80 = 25%.

--- tarjetas | fa-line-chart | Two kinds of interest
* fa-arrow-right | {{Simple interest|Interest that is always calculated on the amount you borrowed or saved at the start.}} | 1,000 at 6% for 2 years: 60 each year, 120 in total. | It grows the same every year. | igual
* fa-level-up | {{Compound interest|Interest that is added to the balance and then also earns interest. It grows faster and faster.}} | 1,000 at 6%: 1,060 the first year and 1,123.60 the second. | It grows faster and faster. | si

--- paso | fa-tag | Interest is the price of money
If someone lends you 100 and you pay back 110 in a year, you paid 10 in interest. The rate was 10% a year.

In savings, compound interest works for you. In a debt, it works against you: if you don't pay, the debt grows on top of itself.

--- pasos | fa-check-square-o | Do it this week
1. Write down three prices you pay often: rent, food and transportation.
2. Find out what they cost a year ago and calculate how much they went up.
3. In any credit offer, look for whether the rate is yearly or monthly.

--- paso | fa-user | A case in one minute
Rubén earns the same as last year: 650 a week. His rent went up from 700 to 756 a month, and transportation from 120 to 132.

His rent went up 8% and transportation 10%. Even though his pay didn't go down, he has 68 dollars less each month.

Rubén isn't overspending. His prices went up and his income didn't. Knowing this helps him ask for a raise with numbers or look for where to adjust.

> **Before you act, check:** in any credit offer, look for whether the rate is yearly or monthly, simple or compound, and which fees it includes.

--- comprueba
1. What is 7% of 600? || 42, because 600 × 0.07 = 42.
2. Inflation went down from 6% to 4%. Did prices go down? || No. They're still going up, just more slowly.

--- recuerda
- Inflation means prices go up; your buying power drops if your income doesn't go up as much.
- Compound interest helps savings and makes debt more expensive.
- Always check whether a rate is yearly or monthly.

== profundiza

--- tema | fa-percent | Percentages and percentage points
A rise and a drop of the same size don't give the same percentage, because the base changes.

- From 80 to 100: up 25%.
- From 100 to 80: down 20%.

If a rate goes from 3% to 5%, it went up **2 {{percentage points|The direct difference between two percentages. From 3% to 5% is 2 points.}}**. In relative terms it went up 66.7%, because 2 ÷ 3 = 0.667.

--- tema | fa-calculator | Simple and compound interest with numbers
**Simple:** interest = principal × rate × time. The {{principal|The amount you borrowed or saved, not counting interest.}} is what you borrowed or saved at the start.

- 1,000 at 6% a year for 6 months: 1,000 × 0.06 × 0.5 = 30.
- 1,000 at 6% a year for 2 years: 120.

**Compound:** final balance = principal × (1 + rate per period) raised to the number of periods.

- Year 1: 1,000 × 1.06 = 1,060.
- Year 2: 1,060 × 1.06 = 1,123.60.

The difference from simple interest is 3.60 in two years. Over 20 or 30 years the difference is huge.

--- tema | fa-calendar | The rate and the period must match
12% a year is not 12% every month. If a rate is 12% a year calculated monthly, 1% is applied each month.

Over a year, the factor is 1.01 raised to 12 = 1.1268. That is, 12.68% a year.

On real products you'll see two abbreviations:

- **{{APR|The yearly cost of a loan, calculated with official rules. It's for comparing loans.}}:** the yearly cost of credit. Check which fees it includes.
- **{{APY|What your savings earn in a year, counting that interest also earns interest.}}:** what savings earn in a year, with compound interest.

> **Good to know:** loans that charge "only 1% a week" look cheap. But 1% a week compounded for a year is almost 68%. Always ask for the yearly rate.

--- tema | fa-bar-chart | Inflation is measured with indexes
A price index sums up the price of a basket of products. If it goes from 200 to 210, inflation is (210 − 200) ÷ 200 = 5%.

Two months in a row of 1% add up to 2.01%, not 2%, because they build on each other: 1.01 × 1.01 − 1.

When inflation goes down, prices don't go down. They go up more slowly. When prices go down, that's called deflation.

In the U.S. the official index is published by the [Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS)](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/).

--- tema | fa-line-chart | Real return
If your savings grow 8% and inflation is 5%, your buying power grows 1.08 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = 2.86%.

If your savings grow 3% and inflation is 5%, you have more dollars but can buy less: 1.03 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = −1.9%.

--- tema | fa-exchange | Exchange rate
The {{exchange rate|What one currency costs in another. For example, how many pesos you get for one dollar.}} is the price of one currency in another.

Always say which way it goes: 17 pesos per dollar isn't the same as 0.059 dollars per peso.

Keep facts apart from forecasts. A news story about a possible crisis doesn't tell you when your hours will be cut. It's more useful to prepare a plan with less income (you'll see it in M1 U08).

--- tema | fa-comments | How to use these numbers to ask for a raise
If your pay hasn't gone up and prices have, you have an argument with numbers.

Calculate how much inflation went up since your last raise and how much your main expenses went up. For example: "Since my last raise, two years ago, prices have gone up about 7%."

Ask for a conversation, bring your numbers and talk about your work. Mention how long you've been there, what new responsibilities you have and what results you deliver.

If there's no raise, ask about other options: more hours, a different schedule or training.

--- casos
### Case 1. Alex and Mar's rent
General inflation for the year was 3%, but their rent went up 8%.
? Which inflation matters more to them? || The inflation of their own expenses. Rent weighs more than the national average.
? What can they do? || Check how much of their income goes to rent and build their budget with the real increase.

### Case 2. The savings that "grew"
Mar kept 1,000 dollars in an account that paid 3% in a year. The basket of things she buys went up 5%.
? Did she gain or lose? || She has 1,030 dollars, but with them she can buy what used to cost 1,030 ÷ 1.05 = 980.95. Her buying power went down.

### Case 3. The "1% a week" loan
Rubén is offered a "cheap" loan at 1% a week.
? How much is that a year? || Almost 68%, because 1.01 raised to 52 is 1.678. It isn't cheap.
? What should he ask? || The yearly rate (APR) and all the fees, in writing.

--- errores
* Thinking that if inflation goes down, prices go down | You wait for relief that doesn't come | Compare your prices month to month
* Comparing a monthly rate with a yearly one | You choose the more expensive loan | Turn everything into a yearly rate
* Adding or subtracting percentages with different bases | You miscalculate the real change | Always use the earlier price as the base
* Believing that more dollars means more wealth | Your savings lose to inflation | Calculate the real return

# M1 U03 | What do I need to take care of first?
objetivo: Put your priorities in order based on your values and your situation, without blaming yourself.
gancho: Rubén has 60 dollars left until his next paycheck. His mom needs medicine, his work shoes fell apart and a friend invited him to a concert. Everything seems important. In this lesson you'll learn a simple way to decide what comes first.

== esencial

--- paso | fa-random | Choosing is deciding what comes first
There's almost never enough for everything at once. That's why deciding with money means choosing what comes first and what can wait.

Choosing one thing doesn't mean the other doesn't matter. It means you'll do it later or in another way.

--- paso | fa-exchange | Everything you choose has a cost
When you use your money on something, you stop using it on something else. What you give up is called the **{{opportunity cost|What you give up having or doing when you choose something else with your money or your time.}}**.

One sentence makes it visible: "If I do this, then I put that off."

> **Key idea:** there are no "good" or "bad" expenses for everyone. There are decisions that protect what matters to you.

--- tarjetas | fa-th-large | Three levels of priority
* fa-exclamation-circle | Urgent | It's due soon and has a serious consequence if you don't do it: rent, medicine, getting to work. | It comes first. | no
* fa-star | Important | It gets you closer to a goal, but it can wait a few days: saving, helping family. | Plan it with a date. | si
* fa-clock-o | Can wait | You like it or it's convenient, but nothing happens if you put it off: an outing, a new phone. | Decide it calmly. | igual

--- pasos | fa-list-ol | Four questions to start
1. **What do I need to protect?** Housing, food, health, work.
2. **What commitments do I already have?** Payments, debts, helping family.
3. **What do I want to achieve?** A specific goal.
4. **What worries me?** What keeps you up at night.

--- paso | fa-hand-paper-o | The pause rule
When something seems urgent, ask for three facts: the real due date, what happens if you wait and what other options there are.

Being tired or short on money makes it harder to compare. That's why it helps to set a rule before the offer arrives.

For example: "I wait 48 hours before any purchase over 100 dollars."

--- paso | fa-user | A case in one minute
Daniela gets a message: "Exclusive offer, a TV for 20 a week, today only." Her TV works, but the new one is bigger.

She applies her rule: she waits 48 hours and does the math. Twenty a week is 1,040 a year, and the cash price was 480.

After 48 hours, the offer is still there. Daniela decides not to buy it and to set aside 20 a week for her son's school uniform, due in two months.

> **Before you act, check:** if an offer says "today only," ask whether it will be there tomorrow. The answer is almost always yes.

--- comprueba
1. What is opportunity cost? || What you give up when you choose something else with your money.
2. An offer says "today only." What do you do? || I ask for the real date, ask what happens if I wait and compare other options before deciding.

--- recuerda
- First, what protects your housing, food, health and work.
- "If I do this, I put that off" makes visible what you give up.
- A pause rule protects you from decisions made under pressure.

== profundiza

--- tema | fa-heart | Numbers and values
A money decision combines numbers and values.

Helping your mom, taking a break or calling your family can be important even if it doesn't make money.

The goal isn't to follow a universal list of correct expenses. It's to choose with information and know what you're giving up.

--- tema | fa-columns | The three-option method
For an important decision, write down two real options and the option of waiting. For each one, note: cost in money, time, safety and effect on your family.

For example, doing a paperwork task in person costs 25 in transportation and 60 in lost pay: 85 in total. Doing it online costs 10, but you don't have a secure connection.

Before choosing, ask whether there are appointments at another time or a trusted place with internet, like a public library.

--- tema | fa-bullhorn | Pressures you can recognize
- **The clock:** "today only" creates urgency so you don't compare.
- **What you already spent:** it pushes you to stick with a bad purchase so you "don't lose" what you put in.
- **Comparison:** seeing what others buy changes what you consider normal.

The answer is a pause that matches the size of the commitment. A two-year contract deserves more review than a grocery purchase.

--- tema | fa-shield | When the problem isn't the budget
If someone controls your documents or your income, or forces you into debt, a better budget won't solve the problem.

In that case your safety comes first. You'll see it in M4 U05.

--- tema | fa-users | Deciding as a family
When several people share expenses, each one may have different priorities. That's normal.

It helps to have a short talk at the start of each month: what payments are coming, what each person wants and what can wait.

Some rules that work in many homes:

- each adult has a small amount for personal use, without having to explain what it's for;
- big purchases are decided together;
- nobody signs debts in someone else's name without their permission.

If one person decides everything and the other can't give an opinion, see lesson M4 U05.

--- casos
### Case 1. The two subscriptions
Mar pays 15 a month for a music app she uses every day and 9 for a video app she forgot she had.
? Should she cancel the more expensive one? || Not necessarily. What matters is the value she gets. She doesn't use the video app: that's the one worth canceling.

### Case 2. The birthday present
Alex has 60 dollars left over. Alex can buy their son's birthday present or save the money.
? What is the right answer? || It depends on Alex's priorities. A good decision keeps the bills covered, explains the choice and doesn't call the present "bad" or the savings "mandatory."

### Case 3. Rubén's 60 dollars
Rubén has 60 dollars until his next paycheck. His mom needs a 40-dollar medicine, his work shoes are torn and he was invited to a concert.
? What is urgent? || The medicine and the shoes, because they protect health and work.
? What does he do about the concert? || He puts it off or looks for a no-cost option. He can set aside a little from each paycheck to go to the next one.

--- errores
* Paying first whatever pushes hardest | What protects your home or your job goes unpaid | Ask for the real date and the consequence of waiting
* Calling every personal expense "bad" | Plans that are impossible to keep | Leave room for what gives you well-being
* Sticking with a bad purchase because of what you already paid | You lose more money | Decide based on what's ahead, not on what you already spent
* Deciding on a long contract the same day | You commit without comparing | Apply your pause rule

# M1 U09 | Does filing taxes mean I'll get money back?

== esencial

--- paso | fa-file-text | Filing is not the same as getting money back
**Filing your {{tax return|The document you send the government each year with what you earned, to calculate how much tax you owe.}}** means reporting your information and calculating your tax for the year.

A **{{refund|The money you get back when you paid more tax during the year than you owed.}}** only exists if you paid too much during the year. They're different things.

--- ecuacion | fa-calculator | You paid too much or too little

= 120 | What you already paid
- 100 | What you owed

* fa-gift | {{Tax credit|An amount subtracted directly from your tax. Some give you money even if you owe nothing.}} | It reduces your tax directly. | It lowers your tax dollar for dollar. | si

--- paso | fa-id-card-o | SSN and ITIN
The **SSN** is issued by Social Security. The **{{ITIN|A number the IRS gives people who can't get an SSN so they can pay taxes. It doesn't give permission to work.}}** is issued by the IRS to people who must file and can't get an SSN.

> **Good to know:** if you don't use your ITIN on a federal return for three years in a row, the IRS deactivates it. Renewing it can take months and delay your refund and your state credits, like CalEITC and {{YCTC|California Young Child Tax Credit, for families with children under 6.}}.

--- paso | fa-calendar | Filing and paying have dates
There's a date to file your return and another to pay what you owe. They usually both fall in mid-April.

An **extension to file** usually does **not** give you more time to pay.

It depends on how much you earned, your filing status and how you earned your money.

If you work for yourself, in many cases you must file even if you earned little. And even if you don't have to, filing can get you credits you're entitled to.

Ask at {{VITA|Volunteer Income Tax Assistance: an IRS program that prepares tax returns at no cost for people with low or moderate income.}} or a preparer with credentials whether you have to file this year.

--- tema | fa-bar-chart | Marginal rate and effective rate
In a made-up scale, the first 1,000 pays 10% and the next 1,000 pays 20%.

Moving into the next bracket does **not** make all your income pay the higher rate. Earning more always leaves you with more money.

What's taken out of your wages is an advance on your tax.

--- tema | fa-shield | Watch out for tax scams
Some scams pretend to be the IRS: they call, text or email demanding immediate payment with gift cards or transfers.

The IRS doesn't ask you to pay with gift cards or threaten to arrest you over the phone. If you have doubts, go to IRS.gov yourself.

--- tema | fa-exclamation-circle | If you can't pay it all
If you find out you owe when you file and can't pay it all, file your return on time anyway. Not filing usually costs more than not paying.

The IRS and the FTB have installment payment plans. Request them on their official websites.

--- tema | fa-map-marker | Where to confirm your case
- An immigration attorney or a Department of Justice (DOJ) {{accredited representative|A person authorized by the Department of Justice to give immigration advice at a recognized organization.}}.
- Organizations like *Protecting Immigrant Families*, which post alerts when the rule changes.
- Your county social services office, for each program's requirements.

--- tema | fa-ban | Watch out for "notarios"
Only an attorney or an accredited representative can give you immigration advice. A notary public can't (you'll see this in M4 U02).

This course doesn't give immigration advice either: it helps you ask the right questions.

--- tema | fa-users | Your family's benefits
In many families, citizen children receive benefits in their own right. The 2026 guidance doesn't explain in detail how benefits received by other household members are taken into account.

Bring this question to your appointment: "Do the benefits my children receive count in my case?" Don't take away a benefit your family needs without asking first.

--- tema | fa-comments-o | How to ask without exposing yourself
You can ask general questions without giving your name or your situation. For example: "How is this program taken into account for public charge?"

Legal aid organizations and accredited representatives keep what you tell them confidential.

--- tema | fa-calendar | Check the date of what you read
The public charge rule changed in 2019, in 2022 and in 2026. Many guides online still explain the 2022 rule.

Before you decide, check the date of the information and ask whether it's still current. Legal aid organizations post alerts when something changes.

--- casos
### Case 1. Food for Daniela's son
Daniela's son was born in California. She's thinking about applying for food help and wants to start her residency application next year.
? What does she do before deciding? || She brings her questions to an accredited representative or a legal aid organization, because since 2026 each case is reviewed as a whole.
? What does she ask? || Whether the rule applies to her case and how the benefits she and her son would receive are taken into account.

### Case 2. The neighbor's rumor
A neighbor tells Alex that "any benefit will cost you your green card."
? What does Alex do? || Alex looks for a reliable, recently dated source before deciding, like an accredited representative or a legal aid organization.

### Case 3. Mar's question
Mar is 35, needs medical care and doesn't know if she can enroll in Medi-Cal this year.
? What options does she have while she confirms? || Going to a community health center that charges based on income.

--- errores
* Deciding based on a rumor | You drop a benefit you need or take a risk without knowing it | Confirm with a reliable source
* Asking a notario or "gestor" for advice | You get false information and overpay | Find an attorney or an accredited representative
* Using a guide with an old date | You decide with a rule that already changed | Check the date and ask whether it's still current
* Skipping care out of fear | Your health gets worse | Use a community health center

# M1 U14 | How do I turn what I learned into something I can actually do?
objetivo: Build a 90-day plan with specific actions, dates and a backup if something changes.
gancho: Rubén finished the module with lots of ideas: make a calendar, save, check his taxes. A week later he hadn't done anything. It wasn't lack of will: he hadn't turned them into steps. In this lesson you'll learn to make a plan you'll actually follow.

== esencial

--- paso | fa-bullseye | A wish is not a plan
"I'm going to get organized" doesn't say how to start.

"On Saturday I'll write down my paychecks and payments for the next two weeks" does.

The difference is that the second sentence has an action, a date and something you can show.

--- tarjetas | fa-th-large | A goal you can check
* fa-play | Action | What exactly you're going to do. | "Write down my paychecks and payments." | si
* fa-calendar | Date | When you'll do it. | "On Saturday." | si
* fa-camera | Evidence | What you can show when you're done. | "My two-week calendar." | si

--- pasos | fa-list-ol | Three steps
1. **Choose one specific problem**, for example: "I'm short on money before rent."
2. **Write down one small action and a date.**
3. **Keep a sample** of what you did.

--- paso | fa-search | "I need to find out" counts too
Finding out is a valid action if it has a date.

For example: "On the 15th I'll call {{VITA|Volunteer Income Tax Assistance: an IRS program that prepares tax returns at no cost for people with low or moderate income.}} to ask whether I qualify for CalEITC."

--- paso | fa-refresh | If something changes, adjust
If you lose work hours, the right action may be to ask for help or renegotiate a payment.

Don't keep an impossible savings amount just so the plan "looks good."

> **Key idea:** a plan that adjusts is a plan that works.

--- paso | fa-user | A case in one minute
Daniela wrote her 90-day plan with only three actions. Write down her payments every night. Set up envelopes for home, business and taxes. And make a VITA appointment in January.

After 30 days she checked her progress. She had done two actions, and the third wasn't due yet. Seeing her money in order gave her peace of mind. So she added a new action: set aside 10 dollars per order for her savings cushion.

--- pasos | fa-check-square-o | Do it this week
1. Write three actions with dates for the next 90 days.
2. Set a reminder on your phone to review your plan in 30 days.
3. Tell someone you trust about your plan.

--- paso | fa-comments | Share your plan
Telling a trusted person about your plan helps you follow it. It can be your partner, a friend or someone from the course.

Pick a date to review together how it's going. It isn't about someone watching you, but about having support.

--- comprueba
1. What does a goal you can check have? || Action, date and evidence.
2. Your income drops. Do you have to keep saving the same amount? || Not necessarily. Adjust the plan to your new reality.

--- recuerda
- Every goal needs an action, a date and evidence.
- A few well-chosen priorities.
- Review and adjust your plan every 30 days.

--- tema | fa-check-square-o | Consistency check
Before you call your plan done, check:

- did you add gross and net pay from the same wages?
- did you count transfers between your accounts as income?
- do taxes and remittances have dates?
- did you assign the same savings to two things?
- does the plan depend on one-time income?

--- tema | fa-line-chart | Measure your progress
When you started the course you took, or can take, the CFPB financial well-being questionnaire.

Take it again every 90 days. It isn't about having more money, but about feeling more in control and less stressed.

> **Good to know:** writing down a goal with a date and sharing it with someone you trust makes you more likely to reach it.

--- tema | fa-trophy | Celebrate your progress
Changing the way you handle money takes time. Every action you complete counts.

Write down in your plan what you've already done, even if it seems small: "I made my first calendar," "I canceled a subscription," "I went to VITA."

Seeing your progress encourages you to keep going. And if something didn't work, write that down too: it's information to adjust, not a failure.

--- tema | fa-life-ring | If you need help
If your plan shows a shortfall you can't cover, don't try to solve it alone.

Look for county support programs, community organizations and no-cost financial counseling. The "Where to find help" chapter in the support materials has a list.

In Module 2 you'll learn to choose where to keep your money and to plan what you send to your family.

The calendar and budget from this module will help you decide how much you can send and when, without coming up short.

--- errores
* Writing goals without dates | They never start | Give each action a day
* Choosing too many priorities | You get overwhelmed and give up | Start with three
* Keeping an impossible savings amount | The plan breaks | Adjust to your real income
* Not reviewing the plan | You follow a plan that no longer works | Review it every 30 days
