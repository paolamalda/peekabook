Capstone cases

Five cases that bring together what you learned in each module. Solve them before opening the key.

The capstone cases use made-up data so you can practice without sharing personal information. Amounts without a currency are in dollars. Solve each case before reading the key.

E1. Alex and Mar's calendar (Module 1)

Data. Between Alex's and Mar's pay, the household receives 600 at the start of each week for eight weeks. Mar also receives 200 from food orders at the start of weeks 2, 4, 6 and 8. The household's starting balance is 200.

  • Every week: 220 for food and transportation.
  • Weeks 1 and 5: rent of 1,000.
  • Weeks 2 and 6: utilities of 100.
  • Weeks 3 and 7: card payment of 80.
  • Weeks 4 and 8: insurance of 120 and a remittance of 150.

Income arrives before the payments of the same week. It's net income, with no taxes pending.

Tasks.

  1. Calculate each week and explain the shortfall.
  2. Design an alternative without making up income.
  3. Suppose pay drops to 480 a week in weeks 5 to 8 and recalculate.
  4. Classify the expenses as essential, predictable and family support.
  5. Write three 90-day actions.
See the key

Key.

Week Starting balance Income Outflows Ending balance
1 200 600 1,220 −420
2 −420 800 320 60
3 60 600 300 360
4 360 800 490 670
5 670 600 1,220 50
6 50 800 320 530
7 530 600 300 830
8 830 800 490 1,140

Totals: income 5,600; outflows 4,660; net change 940; plus starting balance 200 = 1,140.

Week 1 shows 420 of rent without funds. The positive ending balance doesn't erase that problem.

A possible alternative: ask the landlord to split the first rent into 580 (week 1) and 420 (week 2) at no charge, and confirm the agreement in writing. Then the balances for weeks 1 and 2 would be 0 and 60, and the rest don't change. Not paying without an agreement isn't a solution.

With less weekly income (without the alternative), weeks 5 to 8 end at −70, 290, 470 and 660. A new shortfall appears in week 5.

Additional tax practice. A self-employed worker receives 2,000 through a platform and 300 in cash. Her costs for the year are 400. The platform deposits 2,000 in her bank and she moves 1,000 to another account of her own. Result before taxes: 2,300 − 400 = 1,900. The deposits and her own transfer aren't counted again.

E2. Rubén: an emergency and three remittances (Module 2)

Data. Rubén's mother, in Oaxaca, asks for 5,000 pesos for an emergency. Rubén can use at most 310 dollars in total.

Provider Exchange rate Fee Delivery Other costs
A 17.00 5 Next day —
B 16.70 0 Same day —
C 17.10 8 Same day The recipient spends 80 pesos on transportation

You pay from your debit account (no cash remittance tax). Made-up quotes from the same moment.

Tasks. Calculate how much you pay so that 5,000 net pesos arrive. Decide whether the date and access change your choice. Then verify a request that came from a new number and write a support agreement.

See the key

Key.

  • A: 5,000 ÷ 17 + 5 = 299.12.
  • B: 5,000 ÷ 16.70 = 299.40.
  • C: (5,000 + 80) ÷ 17.10 + 8 = 305.08.

All fit within 310. A is the cheapest, but arrives the next day. If the need is same-day, B covers it at a lower cost than C. Before sending, verify the urgency and the provider.

Debt variation. You only have 180 left over. A loan would cover B's difference (119.40) with a fixed cost of 18, payable in 14 days: 137.40. If that day you'll only have 100 left over, 37.40 is missing. Don't accept automatically: look at a partial transfer or other real options.

Cash variation. If you paid for B in cash at a store and the transfer were subject to the 1% tax, you'd add about 3 dollars.

E3. Andrés: report and debt plan (Module 3)

Data. Andrés's report shows:

  • Account A: limit 1,000, balance 600, recognized.
  • Account B: balance 900, not Andrés's.
  • Account C: balance 250, even though there's proof of payoff.
  • Account D: a real two-month delinquency.
See the key

Key. A's utilization: 60%. Investigate B as possible fraud and dispute C with the proof. Don't report D as identity theft: it's real. Write two requests (B and C) with the account partially identified, the facts, the proof and what you're asking for. For D, build a plan based on your ability to pay.

Loan comparison. X gives 1,000 with 12 payments of 100 plus 30 upfront: total 1,230. Y gives 1,000 with 18 payments of 75: total 1,350. Y lowers the payment, but costs 120 more. If your monthly margin is 85, X doesn't fit and Y leaves only 10. Looking for another alternative is also a valid conclusion.

E4. Rosa: protection and response (Module 4)

Data. Rosa compares two policies. Policy A: 80 a month, 1,000 deductible and 20% coinsurance. Policy B: 110 a month, 500 deductible and 10% coinsurance. Allowed service: 3,000. Reserve: 400. A message asks for an access code "to process the payment," and the bill includes an unknown 200 service.

See the key

Key.

  • Event with A: 1,000 + 20% of 2,000 = 1,400. With B: 500 + 10% of 2,500 = 750.
  • Yearly premiums: A 960; B 1,320.
  • Event plus premiums: A 2,360; B 2,070.
  • With no event, B costs 360 more a year.
  • The 400 reserve doesn't cover the event with either policy.
  • Don't share the code. Verify the contact and ask for a breakdown of the unknown charge.
  • B isn't better for everyone: it depends on risk and ability to pay.

Additional task. Build the table of contents of a family preparedness plan for Alex and Mar with what you learned in M4 U10 and M5 U08, using made-up data.

E5. Daniela: future and consistency (Module 5)

Data. Daniela has liquid assets of 2,000; a car estimated at 6,000; a car loan of 4,000; a card balance of 500. Basic expenses 1,800 a month. Margin for goals: 120 a month, but she forgot a 40 insurance premium. Goals: reserve, a 600 course in 12 months and long-term retirement.

See the key

Key.

  • Net worth: 8,000 − 4,500 = 3,500. Liquidity: 2,000.
  • Coverage if all the liquidity were available: 2,000 ÷ 1,800 = 1.11 months.
  • Corrected margin: 120 − 40 = 80.
  • The course requires 50 a month; 30 is left for other goals.
  • Don't count the same 2,000 for every goal.

Adverse scenario. Income drops 200 a month: the 80 margin becomes −120. The contributions are no longer sustainable. Review needs, support and dates, and document the gap.

Final result. Explain two decisions with: need, options, information, choice, risk and review date.

Forms for your plan

Fill them in with data from the cases or your own data, on paper or on your computer. Don't upload them to the platform.

F1 Calendar: period; date; starting balance; confirmed income; estimated income; essential expense; debt; tax; remittance; reserve; ending balance; alert; action.

F2 Compared decision: need; option A; option B; amount; total cost; date; access; requirements; risk; source and date; what still needs confirming; decision; review.

F3 Family agreement: purpose; range; date; emergency; verification; review; who takes part; pending items.

F4 Debts: creditor; type; balance; rate; fees; minimum payment; due date; collateral; delinquency; consequence; contact; strategy; next review.

F5 Protection: risk; impact; coverage or control; exclusion; reserve; gap; action; person responsible; date; contact.

F6 Goal: purpose; amount or range; currency; time frame; resources; possible contribution; assumption; adverse scenario; document; consultation; review.

F7 Professional consultation: minimum facts; decision; available documents; specific question; deadline; specialty; confirmed cost; secure channel; answer; next step.

F8 Family preparedness plan: caregiver and backup; location of documents; accounts and payments (without full numbers); authorizations; contacts; review date.
