What being a cosigner means

The lender agrees to make the loan because you're responsible too. This means that:

  • They can collect from you if the other person falls behind, sometimes without trying to collect from them first.
  • Late payments can show up on your credit report, even if you don't use what was bought.
  • The debt counts as yours when you apply for your own loan, and it can lower what you're lent.
  • Getting out of the contract isn't easy. It usually ends only when the debt is paid off or refinanced without you.

Federal law requires many lenders to give you a cosigner notice before you sign. The FTC explains your risks in Cosigning a Loan FAQs.

Good to know: in California, the law requires the cosigner notice to be given in English and Spanish. If you didn't get it, ask for it before you sign. [POR CONFIRMAR]

Ways to help: risks and alternatives

Way to help Who owes Risk to you Safer alternative
Cosigning a loan You and the other person Paying the whole balance and damage to your credit Help with an amount you don't need back
Lending your credit card You Everything spent is in your name Pay directly for one specific purchase yourself
Adding someone as an authorized user You Their spending adds to your debt Set a spending limit or don't add them
Lending cash The other person owes you That they don't pay you back and you come up short on your expenses Lend only what you could give away
Giving your details as a reference The other person None: a reference doesn't sign or promise to pay Confirm they only want your details, not a signature

A reference isn't a cosigner

When someone applies for a card, a loan or a rental, they're asked for references: names and phone numbers of people who know them. Being a reference doesn't obligate you to pay. Under the federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), a collector may only ask you how to locate that person, can't tell you they owe a debt and can't charge you. If they pressure you, write down the details and submit a complaint to the CFPB.

If you decide to lend money to a relative

Agree on these points in writing, even if it's just a message:

  1. The exact amount.
  2. Schedule: how much and when it's paid back.
  3. What happens if a payment is late: is the date changed or paused?
  4. How each payment is recorded: a shared note or receipts.

Key idea: lend only what you could give away without falling behind on your rent, food and debts. That way, if you're not paid back, the relationship suffers, but not your stability.

A remittance you pay for later

Some services let you send today and pay later. If you send 200 today and have to pay 230 in a month, write two events on your calendar:

  • today 200 goes out;
  • in a month 230 goes out.

If your family promises to pay you back 100, don't count that money until there's a confirmed date and ability to pay.

Ask yourself: if I'm not paid back, which bill goes unpaid?

Passwords and access to your accounts

Never share your login information, not even with your family.

Anyone who logs in with your information can move your money, take out loans in your name and change your contact information.

Also, the bank may refuse to reimburse you because you "authorized" the access.

If someone needs to help you review a debt, look at it together on your screen.

Pressure and control

Asking for help is normal. There's pressure or control when someone:

  • threatens you if you don't sign;
  • takes your documents;
  • controls your income;
  • forces you to take on debt.

In those cases, put your safety first (M4 U05). Saying no or negotiating a different amount is part of planning, not a lack of love.

Never use your children's information

Sometimes, out of need, some families open electric, phone or credit accounts using their children's name or number.

That can damage the child's credit history before they turn 18, and will cause them problems when they want to rent or study.

If you find out someone opened accounts with a minor's information, you can freeze their credit at no cost at all three agencies and report it at IdentityTheft.gov.

Cases: think first, then check

Case 1. Mar's cousin

Mar cosigns a car loan of 180 a month because "my cousin will pay it." Four months later, he loses his job.

What happens now?

The lender collects from Mar, and the late payments show up on her credit report.

What should she have done beforehand?

Include those 180 in her budget as a risk scenario. If they didn't fit, the answer was not to sign.

What can she do today?

Talk to the lender before falling behind (M3 U07) and agree on a plan with her cousin in writing. If she has questions about her rights, she can check with the CFPB.

Case 2. The password

A relative asks Alex for the bank password to "manage" a debt.

What does Alex suggest?

Reviewing the information together on Alex's own phone, without sharing the password.

Case 3. The loan to her sister

Daniela lends her sister 500. They agree by message that her sister will pay 100 a month for 5 months. In the third month, her sister can only pay 50.

Why doesn't Daniela fall behind on her rent?

Because she only lent what she could give away.

What do they do?

They adjust the schedule in writing: 50 this month and the rest the next month.

Common mistakes

Signing because "it's just a formality"
You're obligated to pay the whole debt
Read the cosigner notice and calculate whether you could pay
Lending without dates or amounts
Misunderstandings and money that doesn't come back
Agree on everything in writing, even in a message
Counting on money you were promised back
You come up short on your own bills
Count it only when you receive it
Sharing passwords "out of trust"
You lose control of your account and the bank's protection
Review together, without sharing login information
Opening services or credit in your children's name or number
You damage their history before they turn 18
Never use their information; if it already happened, freeze their credit
