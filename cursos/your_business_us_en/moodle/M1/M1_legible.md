# Module 1. Your business and your home: separate money

## M1 U01. Is your business making money or just selling?

**What you will be able to do:** Tell the difference between sales and profit, and figure out whether your business makes money, loses money or just breaks even.

**To start:** Lupita sells tamales and catering orders in Los Angeles: about $4,000 a month. It feels like business is good, but at the end of the month she can't cover her rent. In this lesson you'll see why selling a lot isn't always the same as making money.

### The essentials (5 minutes)

#### Selling isn't earning

Sales are what you charge. Profit is what's left after you pay everything it cost you to sell: ingredients, packaging, gas, mileage, fees and help.



#### Lupita's month

- Sales: **$4,000**
- Ingredients and packaging: **$1,700**
- Gas, mileage, fees and her niece's help: **$900**
- Business profit: **$1,400**
Lupita thought she made $4,000. She actually keeps $1,400, and her taxes also come out of that (M5).


#### Three possible results

| Type | Description | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| You make money | Sales are higher than all costs. | You can pay yourself and save. |
| You break even | Sales equal costs. | You work for free. |
| You lose money | Costs are higher than sales. | You pay out of pocket. |

#### A case in one minute

Lupita kept every receipt for a month. She found that delivery gas and corn husks were almost $400 she never counted. Now she knows her real profit is $1,400.

> **Key idea:** don't measure your business by what you sell, but by what you keep after paying everything.



#### Check your understanding

1. What is the difference between sales and profit?
*Answer:* Sales are what you charge; profit is what's left after paying all costs.

2. If you sell $2,000 and spend $2,000, how much do you earn?
*Answer:* Nothing: you break even and work for free.


#### Remember

- Sales minus costs is profit.
- Count every cost, even the small ones.
- Your taxes also come out of profit.



### Go deeper (5 more minutes)

#### Costs people forget

| Cost | Why it gets forgotten |
|---|---|
| Gas and delivery miles | You pay them out of pocket |
| Packaging, bags, labels | Small amounts that add up |
| App and card fees | They are taken out automatically |
| Your phone and internet | You also use them at home |
| What goes bad | It doesn't look like an expense |



#### Your time costs too

If you work 50 hours a week and the business leaves you $1,400 a month, you earn about $7 an hour: less than minimum wage. Comparing helps you decide what to improve: prices, costs or sales.



#### Cases


**Case 1. Lupita's month**

Lupita sold more than ever in December thanks to tamales, but she paid more for ingredients and help.
- *What does she do to know whether she made money?* She subtracts all the month's costs from her sales before celebrating.


**Case 2. Daniela's orders**

Daniela sold 90 pairs of earrings in a month, but paid for shipping she didn't charge.
- *What does she check?* How much she keeps per piece after materials, shipping and fees.


**Case 3. Javier's hours**

Javier charges well for each painting job, but spends many unpaid hours driving and giving estimates.
- *What does he do?* He figures out how much he earns per hour, counting travel, and adjusts his rate.


#### Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Measuring by sales | You think you earn | Subtract all costs |
| Forgetting small costs | Inflated profit | Keep receipts for a month |
| Not counting your time | You work cheap | Figure it per hour |
| Forgetting taxes | April surprise | Set some aside (M5) |

### Practice

#### Interactive activity

**What would you do? (H5P):** three situations from this lesson with Lupita, Daniela and Javier. Pick the best decision in each one; if you miss, you can try again. It earns experience points.



#### Quiz

1. You sell $3,000 and your costs are $2,200. How much do you earn? a) $800 · b) $3,000 · c) $5,200
2. What is profit? a) Everything you charge customers in the month, in cash and through apps · b) What's left after paying all costs · c) What's in your account at closing
3. Your sales and costs are the same. What happens? a) You earn a little, but the business does leave you something · b) You lose money every month · c) You break even: you work for free
**Answers:** 1-a: $3,000 minus $2,200. 2-b: sales minus costs. 3-c: nothing is left for you.



#### Put it into practice

You sell $2,500 a month. You spend $1,100 on materials, $300 on gas and $100 on fees. How much do you earn?
**Answer:** $2,500 minus $1,500: $1,000.



#### Your plan

This week, write down all your sales and keep every business receipt, even small ones.

- *Profit:* what is left from sales after paying all the business costs.

## M1 U02. Keep business money separate from home money

**What you will be able to do:** Separate business money from household money with a separate account and clear rules.

**To start:** Lupita gets paid by Zelle and in cash into the same account where her part-time paycheck lands. She pays rent and ingredients from there. In this lesson you'll see how to separate without making it complicated.

### The essentials (5 minutes)

#### Two pockets, two owners

Your business and your home are two different pockets. If you mix them, you'll never know whether your business makes money, and at tax time it will be hard to prove your expenses. The rule: **business money only goes out for business things or for your salary.**



#### How to separate

1. Open a checking account just for the business (it can be in your name).
2. All payments go in there: cash, cards and payment apps.
3. Pay business materials, permits and gas only from there.
4. For your home, pay yourself a fixed salary (M1 U03).



#### What goes in each pocket

| Type | Description | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Business | Ingredients, materials, fees, permits. | Paid from the business account. |
| Home | Rent, food, school. | Paid from your salary. |
| Mixed | Everything in one account. | You don't know if you earn. |
| Payments to your personal app | They get lost among your expenses. | Use a business account. |

> **Current fact:** the FDIC insures deposits at banks up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category; the NCUA protects the same at credit unions. Accessed September 29, 2026 through the FDIC and NCUA.

#### A case in one minute

Lupita opened a second checking account at a credit union, with no monthly fee, and linked her payment app to it. Now she knows how much she sells each week.

> **Key idea:** separate the money and pay yourself a salary; you'll know whether your business makes money and your expenses will be ready for taxes.



#### Check your understanding

1. Why separate the money?
*Answer:* To know whether your business makes money and to have proof of your expenses for taxes.

2. How do you move money from the business to your home?
*Answer:* With a fixed salary, not by paying household bills from the business.


#### Remember

- Two pockets: business and home.
- Every payment goes into the business.
- Only your salary goes home.



### Go deeper (5 more minutes)

#### Personal or business account?

If you work as a sole proprietor, you can use a second personal account just for the business. Many business accounts ask for an EIN or business documents (M5 U02). Compare monthly fees, minimum balance and how many transactions are included. Some banks and credit unions open accounts with an ITIN.



#### Cash

If you collect a lot of cash, count it at closing, write down the total and deposit it often. Cash is also income you must report.



#### If you have to borrow

If one day you use business money for a household emergency, write it down as a loan and pay it back.



#### Cases


**Case 1. Lupita's account**

Lupita receives business payments and her paycheck in the same account.
- *What does she do?* She opens an account just for the business and links her payments there.


**Case 2. Javier's supplies**

Javier buys paint with his household debit card.
- *What does he do?* He pays from the business account and keeps the receipt.


**Case 3. Don Ramón's cash**

Don Ramón keeps the food truck's cash at home for weeks.
- *What does he do?* He counts it, writes it down and deposits it often in the business account.


#### Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| One single account | Everything gets mixed | Separate account |
| Paying home bills from the business | You don't know if you earn | Fixed salary |
| Cash not written down | Unrecorded income | Write down and deposit |
| Loans not written down | Holes in the books | Write down and repay |

### Practice

#### Interactive activity

**What would you do? (H5P):** three situations from this lesson with Lupita, Javier and Don Ramón. Pick the best decision in each one; if you miss, you can try again. It earns experience points.



#### Quiz

1. Where do you pay business materials from? a) Your household account · b) The business account · c) Whatever you have that day in your wallet or cash box
2. You use $200 from the business for a household emergency. What do you do? a) Forget it, it's your business anyway · b) Cover it with your credit card and forget about it · c) Write it down as a loan and pay it back
3. How much does the FDIC insure per depositor, per bank, per ownership category? a) $250,000 · b) $25,000 · c) Everything you have, no limit
**Answers:** 1-b: each pocket pays its own. 2-c: that way it doesn't get lost. 3-a: per depositor, bank and category.



#### Put it into practice

In one week $900 came into the business account and you paid $520 for materials. How much is left in the business?
**Answer:** $380.



#### Your plan

This week, choose your business account and link your payment apps to it.

- *FDIC:* federal agency that insures bank deposits.

## M1 U03. Pay yourself a salary

**What you will be able to do:** Set a salary for yourself that the business can afford, setting money aside for taxes first.

**To start:** Lupita already separated her money, but she doesn't know how much to take home. Some weeks she takes a lot and others nothing, and she worries about what she'll owe in taxes. In this lesson you'll see how to set a salary.

### The essentials (5 minutes)

#### You work for your business too

If you work in your business, you deserve a fixed owner's salary, on the same date. That way your home has a steady income and the business keeps what it needs.



#### How to set it

1. Figure out your average profit for the last 3 months.
2. Set aside taxes first (a practical rule: 25% to 30% of profit; M5 U03).
3. From what's left, take part as your salary and leave the rest as a reserve.
4. Pay it on the same day and review it every 3 months.



#### Lupita's salary

- Average monthly profit: **$1,400**
- Set aside for taxes (25%): **$350**
- Business reserve: **$150**
- Monthly salary (about $225 a week): **$900**


#### A case in one minute

Lupita transfers $225 every Monday from the business account to hers. She keeps the tax money in a savings account to make her quarterly payments (M5 U04).

> **Key idea:** taxes first, then reserve, then your fixed salary; that way neither your home nor the IRS catches you off guard.



#### Check your understanding

1. Where does your salary come from?
*Answer:* From average profit, after setting aside taxes and a reserve.

2. Why set aside taxes first?
*Answer:* Because as an owner nobody withholds taxes for you: you pay them yourself.


#### Remember

- Taxes, reserve, salary.
- Based on average profit.
- Review it every 3 months.



### Go deeper (5 more minutes)

#### If you are a sole proprietor

As a sole proprietor, your "salary" is not a business expense for the IRS: you pay taxes on all the profit, whether you take it out or not. That's why setting taxes aside is so important. If you have an LLC or another structure, the rules may change (M5 U01).



#### Your salary in numbers

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Average profit, 3 months | |
| Taxes (25% to 30%) | |
| Reserve | |
| Monthly salary | |
| Weekly salary | |



#### If family helps

If your niece or your partner works in the business, agree on pay. If they are employees, payroll rules apply (M8 U01).



#### Cases


**Case 1. Lupita's great week**

Lupita sold three times as much in Christmas week.
- *What does she do?* She pays herself her usual salary and sets the extra aside for taxes and the reserve.


**Case 2. Javier's months**

Javier earns $6,000 in summer and $2,000 in winter.
- *What does he do?* He pays himself a salary based on his average and saves what he earns in good months.


**Case 3. Daniela's taxes**

Daniela spends all her profit and hasn't set anything aside for taxes.
- *What does she do from today on?* She sets a percentage of each profit aside in an account for taxes.


#### Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Spending everything | IRS debt | Set taxes aside |
| Taking money based on sales | Unstable home | Fixed salary |
| Not paying yourself | You don't know your cost | Pay yourself |
| Thinking your salary lowers taxes | Surprise | You pay on profit |

### Practice

#### Interactive activity

**What would you do? (H5P):** three situations from this lesson with Lupita, Javier and Daniela. Pick the best decision in each one; if you miss, you can try again. It earns experience points.



#### Quiz

1. Your profit is $2,000 and you set aside 25% for taxes. How much is that? a) $250 · b) $2,000 · c) $500
2. You had an excellent week. What do you do? a) Pay yourself your salary and set the extra aside · b) Take all the extra home because you earned it · c) Raise your salary starting that day
3. Why do you set aside taxes as an owner? a) Because nobody withholds them for you · b) Because the bank asks you to when you open the account · c) There's no need: they pay themselves
**Answers:** 1-c: 25% of $2,000. 2-a: the extra gets set aside. 3-a: you pay them yourself.



#### Put it into practice

Your profit was $1,800, $2,100 and $1,500 over three months. You set aside 25% for taxes and $200 for the reserve. What is your monthly salary?
**Answer:** Average $1,800; minus $450 and $200: $1,150.



#### Your plan

Figure out your average profit, open an account for taxes and set your salary and payday.

## M1 U04. Records in 10 minutes a day

**What you will be able to do:** Keep a simple record of sales and expenses, keep receipts and do a weekly check.

**To start:** Daniela sells jewelry on Instagram and in an online store. She keeps payments in screenshots and expenses in her head. In April she didn't know what to report. In this lesson you'll see how to keep your records.

### The essentials (5 minutes)

#### What to write down

Every day write down: date, how much you sold (cash, card, apps) and how much you spent and on what. Keep the receipts. The IRS asks you to be able to **prove** your income and expenses.



#### Choose your tool

| Type | Description | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Notebook | Simple. | Add it up every week. |
| Spreadsheet | Adds up by itself. | On your phone or computer. |
| Bookkeeping app | Connects your account and gives reports. | Check cost and data. |
| Screenshots only | They get lost. | They don't add up. |

#### Your weekly check

1. Add up the week's sales.
2. Add up business expenses.
3. Subtract: that's your profit for the week.
4. Compare it with the business account balance.



#### A case in one minute

Daniela made a sheet with date, item, money in and money out, and a digital folder for receipt photos. Every Sunday she does her weekly check. In January her year will be ready to file.

> **Key idea:** write things down every day, keep receipts and check every week; without records there are no decisions and no correct tax return.



#### Check your understanding

1. What do you write down every day?
*Answer:* Date, sales and expenses with what they were for.

2. Why keep receipts?
*Answer:* To prove your business expenses to the IRS.


#### Remember

- Daily: date, in, out.
- Receipts kept.
- Weekly check.



### Go deeper (5 more minutes)

#### Your record

| Date | Item | In | Out |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |
| | | | |
| **Week total** | | | |



#### Business miles

If you use your car for the business, write down the date, destination, purpose and miles of each trip. You can deduct them with the IRS standard mileage rate (M5 U03). No log, no deduction.

> **Current fact:** the IRS standard mileage rate for business use is 72.5 cents per mile from January to June 2026 and 76 cents per mile from July 1, 2026. Accessed September 29, 2026 through the IRS (Notice 2026-10 and Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-29).



#### How long to keep them

The IRS recommends keeping your records and receipts at least 3 years after you file your return, and longer in some cases.



#### Cases


**Case 1. Daniela's screenshots**

Daniela has hundreds of payment screenshots from the year.
- *What does she do?* She moves each payment to her sheet the same day and keeps receipts in a folder.


**Case 2. Javier's miles**

Javier drives to give estimates every day and doesn't write anything down.
- *What does he do?* He writes down the date, destination, purpose and miles of each trip.


**Case 3. Don Ramón's app**

A free app asks Don Ramón for his Social Security number and his bank password to "do his books."
- *What does he do?* He doesn't give his password and chooses a trusted tool or a spreadsheet.


#### Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Writing from memory | Expenses get forgotten | Daily |
| No receipts | You can't deduct | Keep them |
| No mileage log | You lose the deduction | Log each trip |
| Giving passwords | Theft | Never |
