# Module 4. Protect your money, your identity and your family

**Module result:** your pause rule, a fraud response protocol, a review of your coverage, a reserve built in stages and your family preparedness plan.

**Lessons:** 11 · **Estimated time:** 10 to 14 hours with practice.

---

## M4 U01. Why is someone insisting that I act right now?

**What you will achieve:** recognize how scams work and verify on your own before acting.

### The essentials

Pressure to pay, share information or keep a secret keeps you from verifying.

1. Stop the action.
2. Find an official contact on your own.
3. Check the request before deciding.

> **Key idea:** you do not need to prove a message is fake to give yourself time to check. **Hurry is the scammer's tool.**

### Go deeper

A scam tries to make you act before you verify. It uses fear, affection, urgency, authority or an attractive gain.

**Phrases that call for a pause:**

- "Today only" or "you have 20 minutes."
- "Don't tell anyone."
- "Pay first to receive."
- "Give me the code you just got."
- "Pay with gift cards, cryptocurrency or an instant transfer."

**Four steps:** stop, identify what they are asking for, **verify through an independent channel** and decide with information.

**Independent** means using a contact you already knew or finding the official website yourself. Never call the number in the suspicious message.

Knowing your name or part of your account number does not prove they are legitimate. A padlock in the browser only means the connection is encrypted, not that the page is honest. A polished website, a paid ad or testimonials prove nothing either.

**Common scams:** fake jobs, rentals that do not exist, fake immigration help (M4 U02), fake tax notices, online romance, "guaranteed" investments and fake family emergencies.

**The overpayment check.** You are sent a check for more than agreed and asked to send back the difference. The money shows as available, but the check bounces days later and you lose what you sent.

### Cases

**Case 1. The job with a check.** An "employer" sends Alex a check to buy equipment and asks him to transfer part of it to a "supplier." Alex verifies the company on his own and checks with the bank before moving money. The job did not exist.

**Case 2. The bank message.** Mar receives: "Your account will be closed in 20 minutes, send the code." She does not share the code and calls the number on her card.

### Interactive activity

**Scam or not? (H5P swipe game):** 12 adapted real messages. Swipe left if it is a scam and right if it is legitimate, and learn the key sign in each one.

### Quiz

1. What number do you call to verify? a) The one in the message · b) One you look up yourself
2. Does the browser padlock prove the page is honest? a) Yes · b) No
3. Will your bank ask for the code you received by text? a) Yes · b) No

**Answers:** 1-b · 2-b · 3-b.

### Practice

A message says your bank will close your account in 20 minutes if you do not provide a code. What do you do?

**Answer:** you do not share the code or use the link; you call the bank at a number you know. If you already shared it, follow the protocol in M4 U04 right away.

### For your plan

Write your pause rule (for example, "I never pay or give codes on the first call") and two contacts for verifying.

**Key words:** *Scam:* deception to get money or information. *Phishing:* fake messages or pages that ask for information. *Independent verification:* confirming through a channel you found yourself.

**Sources:** [S22] FTC, IdentityTheft.gov.

## M4 U02. Who can really help me with an immigration matter?

**What you will achieve:** recognize the fraud that most affects immigrants (notarios, fake lawyers and government impersonators) and know where to verify.

### The essentials

In the United States, a "notario público" is **not** a lawyer and cannot give you immigration advice.

1. Ask whether the person is an attorney or a representative accredited by the Department of Justice.
2. Verify their license or accreditation in the official source.
3. Never pay for "guaranteed" fixes.

> **Key idea:** only two kinds of people can give you immigration advice: **licensed attorneys** and **Department of Justice (DOJ) accredited representatives** at recognized organizations.

### Go deeper

**Notario fraud.** In many Latin American countries, a notario is a legal professional. In the United States, a "notary public" only certifies signatures. In California it is illegal to advertise as a "notario" to offer immigration services.

**Immigration consultants.** In California they can exist, but only for tasks that are **not legal advice**: translating, filling out forms with the information you give or getting documents. They must register, have a bond and give you a written contract. They cannot tell you which form to fill out or which option is best for you.

**How to verify:**

- **Attorney:** look up their name and license number with the State Bar of California (or the state where they are licensed).
- **Accredited representative:** check the official DOJ list (EOIR) of recognized organizations and accredited representatives.
- **Consultant:** verify their registration with the California Secretary of State.

**Government impersonators.** People who call, write or visit saying they are from ICE, USCIS, the IRS or the police:

- Demand immediate payment to "avoid deportation," "release a relative" or "cancel a fine."
- Ask you to pay with gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers or apps.
- Threaten arrest if you hang up.

Government agencies do **not** ask for payments by phone with gift cards or cryptocurrency. Hang up and verify on the official website.

**Other common frauds:**

- "Work permits," "visas" or "new programs" sold on social media.
- Job offers abroad with a guaranteed visa in exchange for a payment.
- Government forms that are sold (most can be downloaded free at USCIS.gov).

**Warning signs:**

- They guarantee the result.
- They charge for free forms.
- They ask you to sign blank documents or documents with false information.
- They keep your original documents.
- They do not give you a contract or receipt.

**Where to report:** the State Bar of California, the California Attorney General and the FTC (ReportFraud.ftc.gov). Reporting helps other families.

### Cases

**Case 1. Mar's neighbor.** An office with a "Notaria – Inmigración" sign promises Mar's neighbor "papers in 6 months" for 5,000 dollars. Mar goes with her to an organization with accredited representatives, which reviews her case for free.

**Case 2. The "ICE" call.** Alex gets a call: his cousin is detained and he must pay 2,000 in gift cards to get him released. Alex hangs up, calls his cousin and the family: his cousin is at work.

### Interactive activity

**Can they help me? (H5P cards):** review profiles (notary, consultant, attorney, accredited representative, social media "helper") and decide what each one can and cannot do.

### Quiz

1. Can a notary public give you immigration advice? a) Yes · b) No
2. Where do you check an attorney's license in California? a) State Bar · b) In their ad
3. Will ICE ask you to pay with gift cards? a) Yes · b) No

**Answers:** 1-b · 2-a · 3-b.

### Practice

Write three questions to ask before hiring help with an immigration matter.

**Suggested answer:** Are you an attorney or a DOJ-accredited representative? What is your license number or your recognized organization? Will you give me a written contract with the total cost and what it includes?

> **Before you act, check:** the license with the State Bar or the accreditation on the DOJ list. This course does **not** give immigration advice.

### For your plan

Save the contact for an organization with accredited representatives or a certified legal service near you.

**Key words:** *Notary public:* a person who certifies signatures; not a lawyer. *Accredited representative:* a person authorized by the DOJ to give immigration advice at a recognized organization. *Immigration consultant:* can do non-legal tasks if registered.

**Sources:** [R23] State Bar of California · [R37] California Secretary of State, immigration consultants · [R38] DOJ EOIR, accredited representatives · [R47] California Attorney General, immigration services fraud · [R51] FTC, ReportFraud.

## M4 U03. What protects my accounts besides a password?

**What you will achieve:** protect your access, your devices and how you recover your accounts.

### The essentials

An account can ask for a second check besides the password (**multifactor authentication**).

1. Review how you sign in to your accounts.
2. Review how you would recover access if you lose your phone.
3. Keep your important contacts somewhere safe.

> **Key idea:** protect your **email** and your **phone number** first: they are used to recover all your other accounts.

### Go deeper

**Five basic controls:**

1. Different passwords for email and bank (a trusted password manager helps).
2. Multifactor authentication turned on.
3. Recovery email and phone up to date.
4. Phone locked and updated.
5. Bank alerts turned on.

**Never share one-time codes** with someone who calls you, even if they say they are from the bank.

**Remote access.** Do not install apps someone asks you to install during an unexpected "support" call. They can use them to see and control your phone or computer.

**Shared devices:** sign out and do not save passwords.

**Fake voices and videos.** Artificial intelligence can imitate a relative. Agree on a way to verify emergencies with a known contact and a second check.

**App permissions.** Before connecting an app, review what it can see, change and store, and how to remove its access. A budgeting app does not need your contacts or your messages. Allowing it to read your transactions is not allowing it to sell your data.

**If you lose your phone:** protect your line with your phone company, change your email password and notify your bank from another device. Keep a list of contacts outside your phone.

**If there is abusive control at home,** changing passwords all at once can increase the risk. Look for a safe route first (M4 U05).

### Cases

**Case 1. The same password.** Alex used the same password for his email and his bank. An online store's password leaked and someone got into his email. Changing only the bank password was not enough: the email was still compromised.

**Case 2. Tech support.** A window on Mar's computer says "virus detected, call this number." They ask her to install an app. Mar closes the browser and does not call.

### Interactive activity

**Security checkup (H5P checklist):** check the controls you already have and get your next three steps.

### Quiz

1. Which account do you protect first? a) Social media · b) Main email
2. Do you share the code you received if "the bank" calls? a) Yes · b) No
3. Does a budgeting app need to read your messages? a) Yes · b) No

**Answers:** 1-b · 2-b · 3-b.

### For your plan

Complete your five-control checklist and your plan if you lose your phone. The course reviews your plan, never your passwords.

**Key words:** *Multifactor authentication:* a second check besides the password. *Remote access:* controlling a device from a distance. *Credentials:* username and password.

## M4 U04. What do I do if I already shared information or see something strange?

**What you will achieve:** respond quickly and in order to fraud or identity theft.

### The essentials

First describe what happened: lost card, unknown access, a payment you were tricked into or an account you did not open.

1. Contact the institution quickly through a verified channel.
2. Write down the facts, the date and the response.
3. Follow the right route and keep the reference number.

> **Key idea:** act fast. Reporting deadlines matter.

### Go deeper

**Sort the problem:**

| What happened | First action |
|---|---|
| You lost your card | Lock it in the app or by phone |
| Unauthorized access to an account | Change passwords from a safe device and notify the institution |
| You paid because you were tricked | Notify the bank or app immediately; recovery is harder |
| A new account in your name | Report it at IdentityTheft.gov and freeze your credit |
| A wrong charge | File a dispute with the issuer |

**General steps:**

1. Stop talking to the suspect.
2. From a safe device, contact the bank or provider through its official channel.
3. Describe the transaction, date, amount and what you did or did not authorize.
4. Ask for protective measures and a reference number.
5. Keep evidence: messages, receipts, addresses, timeline.

**Identity theft.** Use the FTC's **IdentityTheft.gov** to create a recovery plan. Consider **freezing your credit** with the three agencies (it is free): it stops new accounts from being opened in your name, but it does not protect your current accounts or erase debts.

**The second scam.** Be wary of anyone who promises to "recover all your money" for an upfront payment.

If there is immediate physical danger, call 911. Course questions are not an emergency channel.

### Cases

**Case 1. The unknown account.** Alex's report shows a loan he did not request. He documents it, contacts the institution and the agency, follows the IdentityTheft.gov guide and freezes his credit. He does not pay the "cancellation fee" someone asks for by phone.

**Case 2. The shared code.** Mar gave a code over the phone. Ten minutes later she realizes it. She calls the bank, locks her account, changes her password and reviews her transactions.

### Interactive activity

**Order the response (H5P):** put the response steps in the right order for three different incidents.

### Quiz

1. Does a credit freeze protect your existing accounts? a) Yes · b) No
2. Is negotiating with the scammer a necessary step? a) Yes · b) No
3. Where do you create an identity theft recovery plan? a) IdentityTheft.gov · b) Social media

**Answers:** 1-b · 2-b · 3-a.

### For your plan

Prepare an incident sheet and a list of official contacts (without full account numbers).

**Key words:** *Identity theft:* using your information to impersonate you. *Credit freeze:* stops new accounts from being opened in your name. *Reference number:* tracking number.

**Sources:** [S22] FTC, IdentityTheft.gov · [R09] CFPB, credit freeze.

## M4 U05. Is talking about money the same as controlling someone?

**What you will achieve:** recognize economic abuse and know safe support options.

### The essentials

Agreeing on expenses as a couple is not the same as taking someone's pay, keeping them from working or demanding they sign a debt under threats.

1. Identify the behavior (in the made-up case).
2. Think about what support could be requested safely.
3. Avoid actions that increase the danger.

> **Key idea:** controlling another person's money, documents or decisions can be **economic abuse**. Recognizing it does not require you to share your private life.

### Go deeper

**Signs of control:**

- Taking someone's pay or cards.
- Keeping them from working or studying.
- Opening debts in another person's name.
- Keeping their ID documents.
- Threatening them for asking questions or spending.

A disagreement about spending is not automatically abuse. What is worrying is a **pattern** of control.

**Think about your safety first.** A visible change (changing passwords, keeping documents, opening an account) can increase the risk in some relationships. Look for confidential guidance from a safe device at a safe time.

**Older adults or people who depend on care** can also experience economic abuse. Helping manage someone's money requires their permission and limits: having access does not give you the right to use it for yourself.

**To pass the course you do not have to** confront anyone, open a secret account, leave your home or tell your story. You can work with made-up cases.

**Help:** national hotlines and local organizations that support survivors of violence offer confidential guidance in Spanish. In immediate danger, call 911.

### Cases

**Made-up case.** One person pressures their partner to sign a loan they cannot read and asks for their passwords. There is missing information, pressure and the risk of a debt. A safe response: pause, look for independent support and do not keep information where the other person can see it.

### Interactive activity

**Agreement or control? (H5P sorting):** read situations and decide whether they describe a healthy agreement or a pattern of control.

### Quiz

1. Is a disagreement about spending always abuse? a) Yes · b) No
2. To pass, must you confront the other person? a) Yes · b) No
3. Does having access to an older relative's money give you the right to use it? a) Yes · b) No

**Answers:** 1-b · 2-b · 3-b.

### For your plan

You can complete this lesson with a made-up case. No personal story or proof is requested.

**Key words:** *Economic abuse:* control over another person's resources or economic decisions. *Autonomy:* being able to decide about your own money.

## M4 U06. What is the point of setting a little money aside?

**What you will achieve:** build reserves in stages according to their purpose.

### The essentials

An unexpected repair does not wait for the next paycheck.

1. Choose which situation you want to be able to handle.
2. Calculate how much it would cost.
3. Review what you can set aside without neglecting the basics.

> **Key idea:** an **emergency fund** is accessible money for the unexpected. It is not money already set aside for rent.

### Go deeper

**Three reserves with different purposes:**

| Reserve | What for | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Operating cushion | Short gaps between paychecks and payments | Rent is due before payday |
| Planned expenses | Known payments that are not monthly | Car registration, school supplies |
| Emergency | What cannot be predicted | Illness, job loss |

They can be in the same account, but write down how much is for each. Do not count the same money in all three.

**How long does it cover me?** Reserve ÷ monthly basic expenses. 600 ÷ 1,500 = 0.4 months (about 12 days). If part of your income keeps coming (900 of 1,500), the reserve only needs to cover the 600 difference each month.

**In stages:** first a small surprise, then a week of expenses, then a bigger goal. If there is no room today, the first step may be to stabilize and look for support.

**Where to keep it:** accessible, safe and without risk of losing value. An investment that can drop or a deposit with a penalty is not a good place for emergency money.

**Using it is not failing.** Write down why you used it and how you will rebuild it.

### Cases

**Case 1. Alex and Mar's reserve.** They have 900 and basic expenses of 1,800: half a month. If expenses rise to 2,000, the same reserve covers 0.45 months.

**Case 2. Car registration.** Mar thought car registration (600 a year) was an emergency. It was a planned expense: 50 a month for 12 months covers it.

### Interactive activity

**Reserve thermometer (H5P):** enter your reserve and expenses, and see how many days it covers in different scenarios.

### Quiz

1. Reserve 600 and expenses 1,500. How many months does it cover? a) 0.4 · b) 2.5
2. Is a known annual payment an emergency? a) Yes · b) No, it is a planned expense
3. Is an investment that can drop a good place for the reserve? a) Yes · b) No

**Answers:** 1-a · 2-b · 3-b.

### For your plan

Define your first stage, where you will keep it, when you will use it and how you will rebuild it. No amount is required to pass.

**Key words:** *Emergency fund:* money for the unexpected. *Sinking fund:* money for known payments. *Liquidity:* how quickly you can use a resource.
