## M1 U01. Is all the money I receive money I earned?

**What you will achieve:** tell apart money you earn, money you borrow, money you only move and money that is returned to you.

### The essentials

You have 100 dollars and someone lends you 50. Now you can use 150, but you still owe 50.

1. Count what you have: 150.
2. Count what you owe: 50.
3. Subtract: 150 − 50 = 100. That is what is really yours.

> **Key idea:** a loan gives you money to use today, but it does not make you richer. What you have minus what you owe is called **net worth**.

### Go deeper

Money does three things: it lets you exchange (buy and sell), it puts a price on things and it stores buying power for later.

Money is not the same as wealth. A person can own a house and have almost no cash for an emergency. It is not the same as income either: if you borrow 500, your cash goes up 500 and your debt also goes up 500. Your net worth did not change.

Money takes several forms. A bill is **cash**. A bank balance is a **deposit**: the institution owes you that money. A card is only a tool to use your deposit (debit) or a loan (credit). An app can show you several different balances on the same screen. Before using it, find out who actually holds your money and which contract protects it.

**Two questions for every money movement:**

1. Did the money I have available change?
2. Did what I own minus what I owe change?

| Movement | Does cash change? | Does net worth change? |
|---|---|---|
| You get your paycheck | Goes up | Goes up |
| You borrow money | Goes up | No change (debt goes up too) |
| You pay part of a debt (principal) | Goes down | No change (debt goes down too) |
| You pay interest | Goes down | Goes down |
| You move money from one of your accounts to another | Total does not change | No change |
| You get a refund on a purchase | Goes up | Only reverses an earlier expense |
| You buy a tool | Cash goes down | You swap cash for an item; if it loses value, net worth goes down |

If you work for yourself, what you collect is not all profit either: part of it pays back what you spent on materials or merchandise.

**Guided walk-through:**

- You start with 500 in cash and no debt. You earn 300: you have 800.
- You buy a 200 tool that is worth that much: cash 600 + tool 200 = 800.
- You spend 50 on food: you have 750.
- You borrow 100: cash 650, tool 200, debt 100. Net worth: 650 + 200 − 100 = 750. More cash, not more wealth.
- If the tool is later worth 120, your net worth drops 80 even though you spent nothing that day.

### Cases

**Case 1. The advance from the boss.** Alex gets a 400-dollar advance on his pay. That week his account shows more money than ever. Mar wants to use it to buy a refrigerator. What should they consider? *Answer:* the advance will be taken out of future paychecks. It is not new income: if they spend it today, the next weeks will have less.

**Case 2. Mar's sales.** Mar sold 600 dollars of tamales in a month. She spent 250 on ingredients. Her profit before taxes is 350, not 600.

### Interactive activity

**Drag and sort (H5P):** place ten movements into four columns: *earned income*, *loan*, *transfer between my accounts* and *refund*. The platform explains each mistake.

### Quiz

1. You borrow 300 dollars. How does your net worth change? a) Up 300 · b) No change · c) Down 300
2. You move 100 dollars from your savings to your checking account. Is it income? a) Yes · b) No
3. You pay 20 in credit card interest. What happens to your net worth? a) Up · b) No change · c) Down

**Answers:** 1-b (you have 300 more, but owe 300). 2-b (it only moved). 3-c (you paid a cost and did not reduce the debt).

### Practice

This month you received: wages of 600, a loan of 200, a transfer between your accounts of 100 and a refund of 40 on a purchase. How much is new earned income?

**Answer:** 600. The loan must be repaid, the transfer only moved your money and the refund reverses an expense. If you counted everything as wages, you could commit to spending money your work did not produce.

> **Before you act, check:** if a deposit does not have a clear source, mark it "to clarify" in your records instead of guessing what it is.

### For your plan

Make a list with four columns: cash, deposits, belongings and debts. You do not need to share real amounts with the course.

## M1 U02. Why doesn't the same money go as far anymore?

**What you will achieve:** calculate percentages, inflation and simple and compound interest, and use them to understand your decisions.

### The essentials

Groceries used to cost 100 dollars. Now they cost 105.

1. Subtract: 105 − 100 = 5.
2. Compare with the old price: 5 out of 100 = **5%**.
3. Ask yourself: did your income also go up 5%? If not, you can buy less.

> **Key idea:** when prices go up in general it is called **inflation**. If your income rises less than prices, you lose **purchasing power**.

### Go deeper

**Percentages.** "Percent" means "out of a hundred." To find 5% of 800, turn 5% into 0.05 and multiply: 800 × 0.05 = 40.

To measure a change: (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100.

- From 80 to 100: (100 − 80) ÷ 80 = a 25% increase.
- From 100 to 80: (80 − 100) ÷ 100 = a 20% decrease. The base changed.

If a rate goes from 3% to 5%, it went up **2 percentage points**. In relative terms it went up 66.7%, because 2 ÷ 3 = 0.667.

**A small loan.** You borrow 100 and repay 110 in one year, with no other charges. The 100 is the **principal**; the 10 is the **interest**; the **annual rate** is 10%.

**Simple interest.** Always calculated on the original principal. Interest = principal × rate × time.

- 1,000 at 6% a year for 6 months: 1,000 × 0.06 × 0.5 = 30. Total: 1,030.
- 1,000 at 6% a year for 2 years: 120 in interest. Total: 1,120.

**Compound interest.** Interest is added to the balance and earns more interest.

- Year 1: 1,000 × 1.06 = 1,060.
- Year 2: 1,060 × 1.06 = 1,123.60.

The difference from simple interest is 3.60. Formula: final balance = principal × (1 + rate per period) raised to the number of periods.

**The rate and the period must match.** 12% a year is not 12% every month. If a rate is 12% a year compounded monthly, each month applies 1%. Over a year the factor is 1.01 raised to 12 = 1.1268. The **effective annual rate** is 12.68%.

You will see two terms on real products:

- **APR:** the yearly cost of credit under official rules. Check which fees it includes.
- **APY:** the yearly return on savings, including compounding.

**Inflation with price indexes.** A price index summarizes the price of a basket of products. If it goes from 200 to 210, inflation for the period is (210 − 200) ÷ 200 = 5%. Two months in a row at 1% add up to 2.01%, not 2%, because they compound: 1.01 × 1.01 − 1.

Inflation dropping from 5% to 3% does not mean prices go down. It means they rise more slowly (**disinflation**). If prices fall, that is **deflation**.

**Real return.** If your savings grow 8% and inflation is 5%, your purchasing power grows 1.08 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = 2.86%. Subtracting 8 − 5 = 3 is an approximation. If your savings grow 3% and inflation is 5%, you have more dollars but buy less: 1.03 ÷ 1.05 − 1 = −1.9%.

**Exchange rate.** The price of one currency in another. Always state the direction: 17 pesos per dollar (MXN per USD) is not the same as 0.059 dollars per peso.

Separate facts from forecasts. A news story about a possible recession does not tell you when your hours will be cut. It is more useful to prepare a lower-income scenario (you will see this in M1 U08).

### Cases

**Case 1. Alex and Mar's rent.** General inflation for the year was 3%, but their rent went up 8%. For them, rent weighs the most. Comparing their own spending basket is more useful than the national average.

**Case 2. The savings that "grew."** Mar kept 1,000 dollars in an account that paid 3% in a year. The basket she buys went up 5%. She has 1,030 dollars, but it buys what used to cost 1,030 ÷ 1.05 = 980.95.

### Interactive activity

**Interest simulator (H5P):** change the rate, the term and the type of interest (simple or compound) and watch the balance change on a chart.

### Quiz

1. What is 7% of 600? a) 42 · b) 4.2 · c) 420
2. A rate goes from 2% to 3%. How many percentage points did it rise? a) 1 · b) 50 · c) 3
3. Inflation dropped from 6% to 4%. Did prices go down? a) Yes · b) No, they rise more slowly

**Answers:** 1-a (600 × 0.07). 2-a (the relative increase is 50%, but it is 1 percentage point). 3-b.

### Practice

You save 1,000 and a year later have 1,030. Prices in your basket rose 5%. Did your balance increase? Did your purchasing power increase?

**Answer:** the balance rose 3%, but your purchasing power fell: 1,030 ÷ 1.05 = 980.95 in last year's money. The example does not include taxes or fees.

> **Before you act, check:** for any offer, whether the rate is yearly or monthly, simple or compound, and which fees it includes.

### For your plan

Write down three prices that went up in your home this year and one action that is within your control.

**Key words:** *Inflation:* a general rise in prices. *Purchasing power:* what your money can buy. *Compound interest:* interest also calculated on earlier interest. *Percentage point:* the direct difference between two percentages. *Exchange rate:* the price of one currency in another.

## M1 U03. What do I need to take care of first?

**What you will achieve:** set priorities according to your values and your situation, without blaming yourself.

### The essentials

You have money for only one of two purchases. One lets you get to work tomorrow; the other can wait.

1. Write down both options.
2. Ask yourself what happens if you postpone each one.
3. Choose and write down why.

> **Key idea:** what you give up when you choose is called **opportunity cost**. Choosing does not mean the other thing does not matter; it means deciding what comes first.

### Go deeper

A money decision combines numbers and values. Helping your mother, paying for a rest or calling your family can matter even if it earns nothing. The goal is to choose with information and know what you give up, not to follow a universal list of "good" and "bad" expenses.

**Four questions to start:**

1. What do I need to protect? (housing, food, health, work)
2. What commitments do I already have?
3. What do I want to achieve?
4. What worries me?

Then sort each decision into **urgent**, **important** or **can wait**.

**The sentence that helps:** "If I do this, then I postpone that." It makes opportunity cost visible.

**When everything seems urgent,** ask for three facts: the real deadline, what happens if you wait and what alternatives exist. Tiredness and lack of money make comparing harder; that is why it helps to decide a rule before the offer arrives, such as "I wait 48 hours before any purchase over 100 dollars."

**The three-option method.** For an important decision, write two real alternatives and the option to wait. For each, note: money cost, time, safety and effect on your family.

Example: going in person to an appointment costs 25 in transportation and 60 in lost wages (85 in total). Doing it online costs 10, but you do not have a secure connection. Before choosing, ask whether there are appointments at other times or a trusted place with internet, like a library.

**Pressures you can recognize:**

- An offer with a countdown ("today only") creates urgency.
- What you already spent pushes you to continue with a bad purchase.
- Comparing yourself with someone who earns more changes what you consider "normal."

The answer is a pause that matches the commitment: a two-year contract deserves more review than a grocery purchase.

**If someone controls your documents or forces you into debt,** a better budget will not solve it. Look for safe support (you will see this in M4 U05).

### Cases

**Case 1. The two subscriptions.** Mar pays 15 a month for a music app she uses every day and 9 for a video app she forgot. Cancelling the more expensive one is not always the best decision. What matters is the value she gets.

**Case 2. The birthday.** Alex has 60 dollars free. He can buy his son's birthday present or save it. A solid answer explains the priority, keeps obligations covered and does not call the present "bad" or saving "mandatory."

### Interactive activity

**Choose your path (H5P branching scenario):** Alex gets a "today only" phone offer. Each decision leads to a different consequence over the next month.

### Quiz

1. What is opportunity cost? a) The price of what you buy · b) What you give up when you choose · c) A bank fee
2. What do you do when everything seems urgent? a) Pay whoever pushes hardest · b) Ask for the real deadline, the consequence of waiting and the alternatives
3. A long contract deserves: a) The same review as a daily purchase · b) More review

**Answers:** 1-b · 2-b · 3-b.

### Practice

You have 60 available for a goal or a gift. Write two options and their consequence over the next month.

**Suggested answer:** you can spend 60, save 60 or split it. A good answer explains your priority and keeps your obligations covered.

### For your plan

Write your three priorities and a pause rule. You do not need to share personal experiences.

### Learn more

Free, official resources to go further (verified September 28, 2026):

- **Financial well-being questionnaire and tools** (CFPB; language: ES/EN): https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/financial-well-being/

**Key words:** *Priorities:* needs or goals you take care of first. *Opportunity cost:* what you give up when you choose. *Budget:* a plan for where your money goes.

## M1 U04. Is getting paid every week the same as every month?

**What you will achieve:** calculate your real income based on pay frequency, ups and downs, and the costs of working.

### The essentials

You get 100 dollars every Friday. A month with four Fridays brings 400; a month with five brings 500.

1. Mark every payday on the calendar.
2. Write how much you expect on each date.
3. Mark as "uncertain" anything that is not confirmed.

> **Key idea:** a monthly average helps you plan the year, but **the real dates** are what pay the rent.

### Go deeper

**Pay frequencies:**

| Frequency | Payments per year | To annualize |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | 52 | × 52 |
| Every two weeks (every 14 days, biweekly) | 26 | × 26 |
| Twice a month (e.g., the 15th and 30th, semimonthly) | 24 | × 24 |
| Monthly | 12 | × 12 |

Careful: in Spanish "quincenal" can mean every two weeks or twice a month. Confirm which one applies to you: there are 2 payments of difference per year.

Example: 1,200 every two weeks = 31,200 a year = 2,600 a month on average. But most months bring two payments (2,400) and two months a year bring three.

**Daily pay:** count the days you actually worked. 100 a day for 20 days = 2,000, not 3,000.

**Variable income:** look at several months and build three scenarios: low, usual and high. If four months brought 1,800, 2,100, 1,600 and 2,500, the average is 2,000, but a fixed commitment of 2,000 would fail in the 1,600 month. Decide what the lowest month covers and what you set aside in higher months.

**Three kinds of income:**

- **Confirmed:** known amount and date.
- **Probable:** depends on shifts, sales or a customer paying.
- **One-time:** does not repeat (a bonus, a big sale).

Do not take on a new fixed commitment just because you had an exceptional month.

**Self-employment:** first subtract the costs of working (materials, gas, tools, app fees) and set money aside for taxes. What remains is what you can use for your household.

**Avoid counting twice:** if a work app deposits your pay and then you move that money to your bank, it is one income.

**Cash:** record what you collect every day and separate business, household and tax money. Keeping it outside the bank does not change what it represents.

### Cases

**Case 1. Alex and the three-paycheck months.** Alex earns 900 every two weeks. In March he gets three paychecks (2,700). If he uses that month as a reference and signs a car loan, the two-paycheck months (1,800) will not be enough.

**Case 2. Mar and the slow season.** In January Mar has fewer houses to clean. If her December income was 2,400 and January's 1,500, she needs a plan for the low month before it arrives.

### Interactive activity

**Payday calendar (H5P):** mark the paydays of a year paid every two weeks and find which months get three paychecks.

### Quiz

1. How many payments per year are "every two weeks"? a) 24 · b) 26 · c) 52
2. You earn 100 a day and worked 18 days. How much did you receive? a) 1,800 · b) 3,000
3. Should you take on a fixed commitment based on your best month? a) Yes · b) No

**Answers:** 1-b · 2-a · 3-b.

### Practice

You earn 150 a day, work 18 days and spend 270 on work costs. What is left before taxes and household expenses?

**Answer:** 150 × 18 = 2,700; minus 270 = 2,430. Taxes and household expenses still need to come out.

> **Before you act, check:** with your employer, the exact pay frequency and the dates for the next three months.

### For your plan

Make an 8-week calendar with your paydays. Use one color for confirmed income and another for estimated income.

### Learn more

Free, official resources to go further (verified September 28, 2026):

- **Financial education tools (includes Your Money, Your Goals)** (CFPB; language: EN, some in Spanish): https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/educator-tools/
- **Gig Economy Tax Center** (IRS; language: EN/ES): https://www.irs.gov/businesses/gig-economy-tax-center

## M1 U05. Why is my deposit smaller than what I earned?

**What you will achieve:** read your pay stub and know when and where to ask for clarification.

### The essentials

Your pay stub shows 500 dollars **before deductions** (gross pay). They deduct 50. 450 reaches your account (net pay).

1. Find how much you earned before deductions.
2. Read each deduction.
3. Check: 500 − 50 = 450.

> **Key idea:** to know how much you can use, count the **net** amount. Do not add gross and net together.

### Go deeper

**How to read a pay stub, from top to bottom:**

1. Pay period and pay date.
2. Regular hours, overtime and hourly rate.
3. Other pay (bonuses, tips).
4. **Gross pay:** the total before deductions.
5. **Deductions:** taxes withheld, health insurance, retirement plan and others.
6. **Net pay:** what is deposited.
7. **Year-to-date totals:** totals since January. They are not this week's pay.

In California, your employer must give you a detailed pay statement. Keep your pay stubs and your own record of hours to compare.

Not every deduction is a tax. Some are for your health insurance or retirement. Your employer's contribution to a retirement plan can appear on the stub without increasing the money deposited.

**Step-by-step check:**

- 30 hours × 22 dollars = 660. Plus a 40 bonus = 700 gross.
- Deductions: 90 in taxes, 25 for insurance and 20 for retirement = 135.
- Expected net: 700 − 135 = 565.
- If 550 was deposited, there are 15 to clarify. It is not an accusation: it could be an adjustment or an error.

**When something does not add up,** prepare a concrete question: pay period, hours you recorded, expected amount, amount received and which document you have.

**Benefits:** when comparing jobs, ask how much the insurance costs you, when it starts and what conditions apply.

**Contractor or employee.** An app or a boss calling you a "contractor" does not by itself decide your legal classification. If you have questions about overtime, breaks, minimum wage or classification, keep pay stubs, schedules and messages and contact the California Department of Industrial Relations (DIR). The minimum wage in your city can be different from the state's.

### Cases

**Case 1. The missing hours.** Alex worked 40 hours, but the stub says 36. Before complaining, he confirms the pay period dates (maybe 4 hours fall into the next one) and compares them with his record.

**Case 2. The unknown deduction.** Mar sees a 12-dollar deduction labeled "uniform." She asks in writing for an explanation.

### Interactive activity

**Interactive pay stub (H5P):** hover over each part of a sample pay stub to see what it means.

### Quiz

1. Which money can you use? a) Gross · b) Net
2. Are all deductions taxes? a) Yes · b) No
3. If they call you a "contractor," does that decide your legal classification? a) Yes · b) Not necessarily

**Answers:** 1-b · 2-b · 3-b.

### Practice

The stub shows 36 hours at 20 dollars and net pay of 600. Your record says 40 hours. What do you ask first?

**Answer:** confirm the pay period dates, compare the 4-hour difference with your record and ask for a breakdown of deductions. Without that information you cannot know the correct net amount.

> **Before you act, check:** the current minimum wage for your city and the state, and the overtime rules, on the DIR official website. Never write your full SSN in a public question.

### For your plan

Create a folder for pay stubs and a record of your hours. Write one question about a benefit you do not understand.

### Learn more

Free, official resources to go further (verified September 28, 2026):

- **Wages and wage claims (Labor Commissioner)** (DIR de California; language: EN/ES): https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/
- **CFPB in Spanish** (CFPB; language: ES): https://www.consumerfinance.gov/es/

**Key words:** *Gross pay:* before deductions. *Net pay:* after deductions. *Withholding:* money deducted and sent, for example as a tax prepayment. *Employer contribution:* money your employer puts into your retirement plan.

**Sources:** [S11] DIR, Know Your Rights.

## M1 U06. Can a small purchase add up to a lot?

**What you will achieve:** measure the real weight of your big and small expenses and choose changes you can actually keep.

### The essentials

You buy something for 3 dollars ten times a month.

1. Write the cost of one time: 3.
2. Count how many times it happens: 10.
3. Multiply: 3 × 10 = 30 a month, 360 a year.

> **Key idea:** a small expense that repeats can add up (**small recurring expense**). That does not make it bad: first ask yourself what it does for you.

### Go deeper

The size of an expense does not decide whether it is necessary. Before judging it, record: amount, frequency, what it is for and whether you can change it.

**Look at the big expenses first.** Rent, transportation, childcare and health usually explain most of the budget. Giving up coffee will not make up for rent that goes up 200 dollars.

**Expenses that are not monthly.** Insurance of 600 every six months is like setting aside 100 a month, even though it is paid on one date.

**Subscriptions.** Check them on your statement and in your app store. Cancelling the card does not always cancel the service: cancel it with the provider and keep the confirmation.

**From avoided spending to real savings:**

- A 4-dollar purchase, 22 times a month, costs 88.
- If you cut it to 10 times, you free up 48 (not 88).
- If on the other 12 occasions you buy something for 1.50, net savings are 48 − 18 = 30.
- That money only becomes savings if you decide where it goes. Otherwise, it gets spent on something else.

**Cost per unit.** Divide the price by the quantity you will actually use. A big package can be cheaper per item but a worse choice if it spoils or leaves another need without money.

**Promotions.** Before accepting one, ask whether you would buy it without the discount and whether there are charges or renewals later.

**No blame.** Do not cut enough food or safe transportation to reach a poorly calculated goal.

### Cases

**Case 1. The sodas at the job site.** Alex buys a 2.50 soda every workday: 55 a month. He decides to bring water 3 days a week and keeps the soda on Fridays. He frees up about 30 a month and assigns it to his reserve goal.

**Case 2. The forgotten subscription.** Mar finds a 12.99 monthly charge from an app she tried "for free." She has been paying for 8 months: 103.92.

### Interactive activity

**Expense hunt (H5P game):** on a sample statement, find the subscriptions, repeated charges and avoidable fees in 60 seconds.

### Quiz

1. A 4-dollar expense, 20 times a month, adds up to how much a year? a) 80 · b) 960 · c) 480
2. Does cancelling the card automatically cancel a subscription? a) Yes · b) Not necessarily
3. Insurance of 600 every 6 months: how much should you set aside each month? a) 50 · b) 100 · c) 600

**Answers:** 1-b (80 × 12) · 2-b · 3-b.

### Practice

Package A: 12 items for 9 dollars. Package B: 20 items for 14. You will only use 10 before they expire. Which do you choose?

**Answer:** B is cheaper per item bought (0.70 versus 0.75). But if you only use 10, the cost per item used is 0.90 for A and 1.40 for B. A costs less money and wastes less.

### For your plan

Choose one expense to reduce, one to cancel and one to keep, and write why.

### Learn more

- **Financial education tools (includes Your Money, Your Goals)** (CFPB; language: EN, some in Spanish): https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/educator-tools/

**Key words:** *Small recurring expense:* a small expense that repeats. *Periodic expense:* a payment that repeats but is not monthly. *Cost per unit:* price divided by the quantity you actually use.

## M1 U07. Why am I short on money if I earn enough this month?

**What you will achieve:** find shortfalls by date, even when the monthly total looks like enough.

### The essentials

You must pay 80 on Wednesday, but your 100 arrives on Friday.

1. Write the 80 payment on Wednesday.
2. Write the 100 income on Friday.
3. Check how much you have before Wednesday.

> **Key idea:** it is not only how much money comes in, but **when** it comes in and goes out. This is called **cash flow**.

### Go deeper

A budget answers "how much." A calendar answers "when." You need both.

**The calendar formula:**

closing balance = opening balance + money in − money out

One week's closing balance is the next week's opening balance.

**What to include:** income, rent, utilities, minimum debt payments, automatic debits, remittances and money you set aside for reserves. A transfer between your own accounts changes where the money is, but it is not an expense.

**From the bank statement to the calendar.** First check that it adds up: opening balance + deposits − withdrawals = closing balance. Then separate:

- Transactions already posted.
- Pending transactions (they may already be subtracted from the available balance).
- Future commitments.

Each commitment goes in only once, on the date the money actually leaves.

**Positive month, negative week.** You start with 100; on day 1 you pay 400; on day 5 you receive 600. The month ends at 300, but on day 1 you are 300 short. A positive balance at the end of the month does not solve day 1.

**Options when a week comes out negative:**

- Set aside money from the previous paycheck.
- Ask to change a due date (and confirm whether it has fees).
- Agree on a partial payment.
- Review an expense that can wait.

A change you requested is not approved until it is confirmed. An overdraft or a loan can cover the gap, but they cost money.

**Adapt the calendar to how you get paid:**

- Daily pay: close each day and add up each week.
- Monthly pay: from day one, set aside what is due later.
- Shared household: agree on what each person contributes and on which date.

### Cases

**Case 1. Rent on the 1st.** Alex and Mar pay 1,800 in rent on the 1st. Alex is paid on Fridays. If the 1st falls on a Monday, the previous Friday's paycheck must cover the rent. They set aside part of each paycheck in the two weeks before.

**Case 2. The automatic debit.** Car insurance is charged automatically on the 3rd. One month, the account had 60 dollars and the 120 charge caused a 35 overdraft fee. Now they set an alert two days before.

### Interactive activity

**Calendar simulator (H5P):** arrange income and payments over 8 weeks and see which week has the first negative balance. Try moving a date and see how it changes.

### Quiz

1. What is the closing balance formula? a) Opening + money in − money out · b) Money in − money out
2. If the month ends positive, can there be a negative week? a) Yes · b) No
3. Is a due-date change you requested already approved? a) Yes · b) No, not until they confirm

**Answers:** 1-a · 2-a · 3-b.

**Answer:** week 1 closes at 70. Week 2 opens at 70 and closes at −50. You must cover or change that shortfall before committing to another expense.

> **Before you act, check:** whether changing a due date causes interest or fees, and ask for written confirmation.

### Learn more

Free, official resources to go further (verified September 28, 2026):

- **Financial education tools (includes Your Money, Your Goals)** (CFPB; language: EN, some in Spanish): https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/educator-tools/
- **Ways to pay your bills** (CFPB; language: EN; Spanish version in the same series): https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_ways-to-pay-your-bills_2022-08.pdf

**Key words:** *Cash flow:* money in and out over time. *Available balance:* what the bank lets you use today; it may not include future payments. *Overdraft:* when you spend more than you have and the bank covers it for a fee. *Due date:* the last day to pay.
