Think about the person receiving

Think about how your family receives the money: what documents they have, whether they have a bank account or a cell phone, how far the pickup point is, its hours and how safe the way there is.

An app you use easily can be hard for an older person.

Good to know: in Mexico, many families can receive remittances directly in a bank account or in a Financiera para el Bienestar account. That way they don't have to carry cash on the street.

Ask without intruding

Ask how much actually arrives and what trouble they have picking it up.

Don't ask for passwords or full control of their money. Your family also has the right to decide.

Small sends or one big one

Sending 75 every week isn't the same as sending 300 once a month:

  • you pay more fees with small sends;
  • your family receives the money at different times;
  • you may come up short between sends if you don't plan.

Compare the total monthly cost and what works for the person receiving.

Limits and independence

A lasting agreement accepts changes: your work can change, and there are emergencies on your side too.

If the conversation brings threats or pressure, put your safety first. You don't have to send what you don't have.

Prepare for foreseeable expenses

Foreseeable expenses, like the start of school, a celebration or a yearly payment, can be planned.

Ask your family what big expenses are coming this year and in what month. Divide the amount by the months left and set it aside little by little.

That way they don't turn into last-minute "emergencies."

When you can't help this month

Sometimes there isn't enough. Saying so early and with care is better than going silent.

For example: "This month my hours were cut. I can send 100 instead of 200. Next month I'll let you know how I'm doing."

If your family lives in a country other than Mexico

Rules and services change by country. In El Salvador, for example, the dollar is used and there's no exchange rate, but there are fees and charges to receive money.

Ask your family how they prefer to receive it and compare the services that reach their city. The World Bank comparison tool covers many countries.

Cases: think first, then check

Case 1. Alex's mom

She asks for 200 dollars every month for food, 120 at the start of her granddaughter's school year and, this month, 400 for a hospital stay.

How does Alex sort it?

Food: regular. School: foreseeable. Hospital: possible emergency.

What does Alex do before deciding?

Verify the hospital amount and the alternatives.

Case 2. Luis's messages

Luis's mom texts him every month with a different amount.

What changes?

They agree on a regular amount on the 15th and to give two weeks' notice if a foreseeable expense is coming.

Case 3. Daniela's sister

Daniela sends money to her sister in San Salvador for her niece's school.

In what currency does she plan?

In dollars, because El Salvador uses the dollar. Even so, she compares fees and how long it takes to arrive.
