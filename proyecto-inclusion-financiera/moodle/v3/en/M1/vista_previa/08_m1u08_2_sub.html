Expenses that aren't monthly

If a 240 yearly payment is due in 6 months and you haven't set anything aside, you need 40 a month until that date.

Dividing by 12 (20 a month) would leave you short. After the payment, 20 a month is enough for the next year.

Car registration, school supplies or renewing a document aren't emergencies: you can see them coming.

If there's a deficit

A deficit is when what you need to pay is more than what you have. Order your actions like this:

  1. Protect basic needs.
  2. Check for support programs and your rights.
  3. Negotiate obligations.
  4. Cut expenses that can be replaced.
  5. Look for realistic extra income, minus its costs.

Don't fill the budget with made-up income, and don't call it "saving" when you put what was missing on a credit card.

Keep an emergency fund

An emergency fund is money for surprises, like an illness or losing work hours.

Start with a small goal, for example one week of basic expenses. Keep it apart from your day-to-day money.

Good to know: according to Federal Reserve surveys, many families in the U.S. couldn't cover a surprise 400-dollar expense with cash or its equivalent. Starting small already puts you ahead.

What about the 50/30/20 rule?

You've probably heard of the 50/30/20 rule: 50% for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for savings.

It can be a starting point, but in cities with high rent almost nobody can follow it. If rent is already 45% of your income, you didn't fail: your city is expensive.

Use it as a reference, not as a test. What matters is that your plan is realistic and that you can keep it up.

Tools for your budget

You can make your budget in a notebook, in a spreadsheet on your phone or with your bank's app, which often sorts your expenses on its own.

What matters isn't the tool, but reviewing it every month and adjusting it when your life changes.

Cases: think first, then check

Case 1. Alex and Mar's hard month

Their income drops from 2,400 to 1,920 and they're 480 short.

What adjustments can they make?

Pause savings (150), agree with the family on a smaller remittance for two months (100) and ask for a payment plan on the debt (100). That adds up to 350.

And the 130 still missing?

Look for support programs and review basic expenses. It's okay if a solution is still missing.

Case 2. Is the subscription the problem?

Canceling a 15 subscription leaves a 465 shortfall.

What does this show?

That the problem calls for reviewing big decisions and looking for support, not blaming the subscription.

Case 3. Daniela's envelopes

Daniela gets income from her hourly job and from her cake orders. She used to mix it all together, and at the end of the month she didn't know what had happened.

What changes?

She sets up three digital envelopes in her bank app: home, business and taxes. Each payment gets split up the same day.
